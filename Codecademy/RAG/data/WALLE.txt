***WALL-E*** (stylized with an [interpunct](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Interpunct "Interpunct") as ***WALL·E***) is a 2008 American [animated](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Animated_film "Animated film") [science fiction film](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Science_fiction_film "Science fiction film")<sup id="cite_ref-5"><a href="https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/WALL-E#cite_note-5">[5]</a></sup> produced by [Pixar Animation Studios](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pixar_Animation_Studios "Pixar Animation Studios") for [Walt Disney Pictures](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Walt_Disney_Pictures "Walt Disney Pictures"). The film was directed by [Andrew Stanton](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Andrew_Stanton "Andrew Stanton"), produced by [Jim Morris](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Jim_Morris_(film_producer) "Jim Morris (film producer)"), and written by Stanton and [Jim Reardon](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Jim_Reardon "Jim Reardon"). It stars the voices of [Ben Burtt](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ben_Burtt "Ben Burtt"), [Elissa Knight](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Elissa_Knight "Elissa Knight"), [Jeff Garlin](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Jeff_Garlin "Jeff Garlin"), [John Ratzenberger](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/John_Ratzenberger "John Ratzenberger"), [Kathy Najimy](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Kathy_Najimy "Kathy Najimy"), with [Sigourney Weaver](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sigourney_Weaver "Sigourney Weaver") and [Fred Willard](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Fred_Willard "Fred Willard"). The film follows a solitary robot named [WALL-E](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/WALL-E_(character) "WALL-E (character)") on a [future](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Future_of_Earth "Future of Earth"), [uninhabitable](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Planetary_habitability "Planetary habitability"), deserted [Earth](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Earth "Earth") in 2805, left to clean up garbage. He is visited by a robot called EVE sent from the [starship](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Starship "Starship") *Axiom*, with whom he falls in love and pursues across the galaxy.

After directing *[Finding Nemo](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Finding_Nemo "Finding Nemo")*, Stanton felt Pixar had created believable simulations of underwater physics and was willing to direct a film set largely in space. *WALL-E* has minimal dialogue in its early sequences; many of the characters in the film do not have voices, but instead communicate with [body language](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Body_language "Body language") and robotic sounds that were designed by Burtt. The film incorporates various topics including [consumerism](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Consumerism "Consumerism"), [corporatocracy](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Corporatocracy "Corporatocracy"), [nostalgia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Nostalgia "Nostalgia"), [waste management](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Waste_management "Waste management"), [human environmental impact and concerns](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Human_impact_on_the_environment "Human impact on the environment"), [obesity](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Obesity "Obesity")/[sedentary lifestyles](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sedentary_lifestyle "Sedentary lifestyle"), and [global catastrophic risk](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Global_catastrophic_risk "Global catastrophic risk").<sup id="cite_ref-Murray_and_Heumann,_2009_6-0"><a href="https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/WALL-E#cite_note-Murray_and_Heumann,_2009-6">[6]</a></sup> It is also Pixar's first animated film with segments featuring live-action characters. [Thomas Newman](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Thomas_Newman "Thomas Newman") composed the film's musical score. The film cost $180 million to produce, a record-breaking sum for an animated film at the time. Following Pixar tradition, *WALL-E* was paired with a short film titled *[Presto](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Presto_(film) "Presto (film)")* for its theatrical release.

*WALL-E* was released in the United States on June 27, 2008. The film received critical acclaim for its animation, story, voice acting, characters, visuals, score, sound design, screenplay, use of minimal dialogue, and scenes of romance.<sup id="cite_ref-7"><a href="https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/WALL-E#cite_note-7">[7]</a></sup><sup id="cite_ref-8"><a href="https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/WALL-E#cite_note-8">[8]</a></sup> It was also commercially successful, grossing $521.3 million worldwide and becoming the [ninth-highest grossing film of 2008](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/2008_in_film#Highest-grossing_films "2008 in film"). It won the 2008 [Golden Globe Award for Best Animated Feature Film](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Golden_Globe_Award_for_Best_Animated_Feature_Film "Golden Globe Award for Best Animated Feature Film"), the 2009 [Hugo Award for Best Long Form Dramatic Presentation](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hugo_Award_for_Best_Dramatic_Presentation#Long_Form "Hugo Award for Best Dramatic Presentation"),<sup id="cite_ref-Hugo_9-0"><a href="https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/WALL-E#cite_note-Hugo-9">[9]</a></sup> the final [Nebula Award for Best Script](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Nebula_Award_for_Best_Script "Nebula Award for Best Script"),<sup id="cite_ref-Nebula_10-0"><a href="https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/WALL-E#cite_note-Nebula-10">[10]</a></sup> the [Saturn Award for Best Animated Film](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Saturn_Award_for_Best_Animated_Film "Saturn Award for Best Animated Film") and the [Academy Award](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Academy_Award "Academy Award") for [Best Animated Feature](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Academy_Award_for_Best_Animated_Feature "Academy Award for Best Animated Feature") with five additional Oscar nominations. The film was widely named by critics and organizations, including the [National Board of Review](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/National_Board_of_Review "National Board of Review") and [American Film Institute](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/American_Film_Institute "American Film Institute"), as one of the best films of 2008,<sup id="cite_ref-11"><a href="https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/WALL-E#cite_note-11">[11]</a></sup><sup id="cite_ref-12"><a href="https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/WALL-E#cite_note-12">[12]</a></sup> and to be considered among [the greatest animated films ever made](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/List_of_films_considered_the_best#Animation_(shorts_and_features) "List of films considered the best").<sup id="cite_ref-13"><a href="https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/WALL-E#cite_note-13">[13]</a></sup><sup id="cite_ref-14"><a href="https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/WALL-E#cite_note-14">[14]</a></sup><sup id="cite_ref-15"><a href="https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/WALL-E#cite_note-15">[15]</a></sup>

In 2021, *WALL-E* became the second Pixar feature film (after *[Toy Story](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Toy_Story "Toy Story")*) to be selected for preservation in the United States [National Film Registry](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/National_Film_Registry "National Film Registry") by the [Library of Congress](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Library_of_Congress "Library of Congress") as being "culturally, historically, or aesthetically significant".<sup id="cite_ref-Tartaglione_16-0"><a href="https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/WALL-E#cite_note-Tartaglione-16">[16]</a></sup> In September 2022, at the request of Stanton, Disney licensed *WALL-E* to [The Criterion Collection](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/The_Criterion_Collection "The Criterion Collection"), who re-released the film as a special edition [4K Blu-Ray](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ultra_HD_Blu-ray "Ultra HD Blu-ray")\-standard [Blu-ray](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Blu-ray "Blu-ray") combo pack on November 22, 2022, marking the first Pixar film to ever receive such an honor.<sup id="cite_ref-17"><a href="https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/WALL-E#cite_note-17">[17]</a></sup><sup id="cite_ref-18"><a href="https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/WALL-E#cite_note-18">[18]</a></sup>

## Plot

In the 29th century, Earth is a garbage-strewn wasteland due to an [ecocide](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ecocide "Ecocide"), caused by rampant [consumerism](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Consumerism "Consumerism"), corporate greed, and environmental neglect.<sup id="cite_ref-19"><a href="https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/WALL-E#cite_note-19">[19]</a></sup>

Humanity is nowhere to be seen, having been evacuated by the [megacorporation](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Megacorporation "Megacorporation") Buy n Large (BnL) to space on giant [starliners](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Generation_ship "Generation ship"), leaving [trash compacting](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Trash_compactor "Trash compactor") robots to clean up the planet, seven hundred years prior. The cleanup has failed and the last remaining active robot, named [Waste Allocation Load Lifter: Earth Class](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/WALL-E_(character) "WALL-E (character)") (WALL·E) has developed a personality. WALL·E remains active by salvaging parts from other inactive robots and lives with his pet [cockroach](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cockroach "Cockroach") as his only companion in a large truck designed to carry the robots.

One day, WALL·E's routine of compressing trash and [collecting interesting objects](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Scavenging "Scavenging") is broken by the arrival of an unmanned rocket probe carrying an egg-shaped robot named Extraterrestrial Vegetation Evaluator (EVE) which is sent to scan the planet for signs of sustainable life. WALL·E is smitten by the sleek, otherworldly robot, and the two begin to connect, until EVE goes into [standby mode](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Standby_mode "Standby mode") when WALL·E shows her his most recent find: a living [seedling](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Seedling "Seedling"). The probe ship eventually returns and collects EVE and the plant, with WALL·E clinging on in fear of losing her. The probe ship then returns to its mothership, the starliner *Axiom*.

In the centuries since the *Axiom* left Earth, its passengers have degenerated into helpless [obesity](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Obesity "Obesity") due to [microgravity](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Microgravity "Microgravity") and [laziness](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Laziness "Laziness"), with robots catering to their every whim. Even Captain B. McCrea is used to sitting back while his robotic [AI](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Artificial_Intelligence "Artificial Intelligence") autopilot helm, nicknamed AUTO, pilots the ship. McCrea is unprepared to receive the positive probe response, but discovers that placing the plant in the ship's Holo-Detector will trigger a [hyperjump](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Warp_drive "Warp drive") back to Earth so that humanity can begin recolonization. When McCrea inspects EVE's storage compartment, the plant is missing, with EVE blaming WALL·E for its disappearance.

EVE is deemed faulty and taken to diagnostics. Mistaking the process for torture, WALL·E intervenes and inadvertently releases all the other faulty bots, causing him and EVE to be designated as rogue robots. Frustrated, EVE tries to send WALL·E home in an [escape pod](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Escape_pod "Escape pod"), but before she can, the two witness McCrea's first mate robot, GO-4, stowing the plant in a pod set to [self-destruct](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Self-destruct "Self-destruct"), revealing that WALL·E did not steal the plant. WALL·E enters the pod to retrieve the plant just as it launches, but both he and the plant survive unscathed. EVE catches up to him and they reconcile, celebrating with a dance in space around the *Axiom*.

EVE brings the plant back to McCrea, who watches her recordings of Earth, concluding that they can and must save it. However, AUTO reveals that he has been programmed with the secret no-return directive [A113](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/A113 "A113") issued after BnL CEO Shelby Forthright inaccurately said Earth could not be saved, also revealing that GO-4 attempted to dispose of the plant on AUTO's instruction. When McCrea countermands the directive, AUTO and GO-4 [mutiny](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mutiny "Mutiny"), electrocuting WALL·E, forcefully putting EVE into standby, throwing them both down a garbage chute and locking McCrea in his quarters. EVE (having been reactivated by a mouse-like robot) and WALL·E are nearly ejected into space with the ship's refuse, but Microbe Obliterator (M-O), a cleaning robot who had been following WALL·E's dirt trail across the ship since his arrival, inadvertently jams the door and saves the two, prompting the Waste Allocation Load Lifter: Axiom Class (WALL·A) garbage bots to abort the ejection. As humans and robots help in securing the plant, McCrea and AUTO fight for control of the *Axiom*. AUTO crushes WALL·E using the Holo-Detector in a last-ditch attempt to keep the human passengers in space; McCrea eventually overpowers and deactivates AUTO by switching him to manual mode, and EVE successfully inserts the plant, initiating the hyperjump.

Arriving back on Earth, EVE quickly repairs WALL·E, but finds that his memory and personality have been erased. Heartbroken, EVE gives WALL·E a goodbye "kiss", which releases a [static electricity](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Static_electricity "Static electricity") shock, restoring him to his normal self. WALL-E and EVE reunite as the *Axiom* inhabitants take their first steps on Earth. Humans and robots turn the ravaged planet into a paradise, and the plant grows into a mighty tree, which WALL·E and EVE rest beneath.

## Cast

### Voice cast

    -   Burtt also voices M-O (Microbe-Obliterator), a cleaning robot.

-   [Jeff Garlin](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Jeff_Garlin "Jeff Garlin") as Captain B. McCrea, the captain of the *Axiom*. (While McCrea's name was never spoken, it does appear on a portrait of him in his quarters. He is only ever referred to as the Captain.)

Loyal only to directive [A113](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/A113 "A113"), him and GO-4 are the only computers not influenced by WALL-E.
-   [John Ratzenberger](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/John_Ratzenberger "John Ratzenberger") and [Kathy Najimy](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Kathy_Najimy "Kathy Najimy") as John and Mary, who live on the *Axiom*, so dependent on their automatic services as to be oblivious to their surroundings. They are brought out of their trances by encounters with WALL-E and [fall in love](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Love_at_first_sight "Love at first sight") with each other.
-   [Sigourney Weaver](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sigourney_Weaver "Sigourney Weaver") as the *Axiom*'s computer. ### Live-action cast

-   [Fred Willard](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Fred_Willard "Fred Willard") as Shelby Forthright, CEO of the Buy n Large Corporation and President of Earth. The only major live-action character, appearing only in videos recorded around the time of the *Axiom*'s launch in the 22nd century. Forthright proposed the plan to evacuate Earth's population and then clean up the planet so they could return within five years. Discovering that Earth had become too toxic to support life, the cleanup and recolonization were abandoned. Forthright issued directive [A113](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/A113 "A113") preventing anyone from returning to Earth. As of 2024, Forthright is the only live-action character with a speaking role in any Pixar film.
-   [Michael Crawford](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Michael_Crawford "Michael Crawford") and [Marianne McAndrew](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Marianne_McAndrew "Marianne McAndrew") appear in an archival recording performing "It Only Takes a Moment" from *[Hello, Dolly!](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hello,_Dolly!_(film) "Hello, Dolly! (film)")*.

## Production

### Writing

> BACK ON M-O AND WALLY \[*[sic](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sic "Sic")*\]
> 
> M-O just finishes cleaning the floor.  
> Wally is fascinated.  
> Impishly makes another mark.  
> M-O compulsively cleans it. Can't resist.
> 
> M-O (bleeps):  
> \[Look, it stays clean. You got that?\]
> 
> Wally wipes the bottom of his tread on M-O's face.  
> M-O loses it.  
> Scrubs his own face.

—Stanton wrote the screenplay to focus on the visuals  
and as a guide to what the sound effects needed to convey<sup id="cite_ref-screenplay_23-0"><a href="https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/WALL-E#cite_note-screenplay-23">[23]</a></sup>

As depicted in the teaser trailer, Andrew Stanton conceived *WALL-E* during a lunch with fellow writers [John Lasseter](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/John_Lasseter "John Lasseter"), [Pete Docter](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pete_Docter "Pete Docter"), and [Joe Ranft](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Joe_Ranft "Joe Ranft") in 1994. *[Toy Story](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Toy_Story "Toy Story")* was near completion and the writers brainstormed ideas for their next projects—*[A Bug's Life](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/A_Bug%27s_Life "A Bug's Life")*, *[Monsters, Inc.](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Monsters,_Inc. "Monsters, Inc.")*, and *[Finding Nemo](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Finding_Nemo "Finding Nemo")*—at this lunch. Stanton asked, "What if mankind had to leave Earth and somebody forgot to turn off the last robot?"<sup id="cite_ref-notes_21-1"><a href="https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/WALL-E#cite_note-notes-21">[21]</a></sup> Having struggled for many years with making the characters in *Toy Story* appealing, Stanton found his simple *[Robinson Crusoe](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Robinson_Crusoe "Robinson Crusoe")*\-esque idea of a lonely robot on a deserted planet strong.<sup id="cite_ref-awn_24-0"><a href="https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/WALL-E#cite_note-awn-24">[24]</a></sup><sup id="cite_ref-avclub_25-0"><a href="https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/WALL-E#cite_note-avclub-25">[25]</a></sup> Stanton made [WALL·E](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/WALL-E_(character) "WALL-E (character)") a [waste collector](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Waste_collector "Waste collector") as the idea was instantly understandable, and because it was a low-status menial job that made him sympathetic.<sup id="cite_ref-world_26-0"><a href="https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/WALL-E#cite_note-world-26">[26]</a></sup> Stanton also liked the imagery of stacked cubes of garbage.<sup id="cite_ref-27"><a href="https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/WALL-E#cite_note-27">[27]</a></sup> He did not find the idea dark because having a planet covered in garbage was for him a childish imagining of disaster.<sup id="cite_ref-screenwriting_28-0"><a href="https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/WALL-E#cite_note-screenwriting-28">[28]</a></sup>

Stanton and Docter developed the film under the title of *Trash Planet* for two months in 1995, but they did not know how to develop the story and Docter chose to direct *Monsters, Inc.* instead.<sup id="cite_ref-total_29-0"><a href="https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/WALL-E#cite_note-total-29">[29]</a></sup><sup id="cite_ref-hauser11_30-0"><a href="https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/WALL-E#cite_note-hauser11-30">[30]</a></sup> Stanton came up with the idea of WALL-E finding a plant, because his life as the sole inhabitant on a deserted world reminded Stanton of a plant growing among pavements.<sup id="cite_ref-ball_31-0"><a href="https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/WALL-E#cite_note-ball-31">[31]</a></sup> Before they turned their attention to other projects, Stanton and Lasseter thought about having WALL-E fall in love, as it was the necessary progression away from loneliness.<sup id="cite_ref-screenwriting_28-1"><a href="https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/WALL-E#cite_note-screenwriting-28">[28]</a></sup> Stanton started writing *WALL-E* again in 2002 while completing *Finding Nemo*.<sup id="cite_ref-usatoday_32-0"><a href="https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/WALL-E#cite_note-usatoday-32">[32]</a></sup> Stanton formatted his script in a manner reminiscent of [Dan O'Bannon](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dan_O%27Bannon "Dan O'Bannon")'s *[Alien](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Alien_(film) "Alien (film)")*. O'Bannon wrote his script in a manner Stanton found reminded him of *[haiku](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Haiku "Haiku")*, where visual descriptions were done in continuous lines of a few words. Stanton wrote his robot "dialogue" conventionally, but placed them in brackets.<sup id="cite_ref-avclub_25-1"><a href="https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/WALL-E#cite_note-avclub-25">[25]</a></sup> In late 2003, Stanton and a few others created a [story reel](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Story_reel "Story reel") of the first twenty minutes of the film. Lasseter and [Steve Jobs](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Steve_Jobs "Steve Jobs") were impressed and officially began development,<sup id="cite_ref-33"><a href="https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/WALL-E#cite_note-33">[33]</a></sup> though Jobs stated he did not like the title, originally spelled "*W.A.L.-E.*"<sup id="cite_ref-34"><a href="https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/WALL-E#cite_note-34">[34]</a></sup>

While the first act of *WALL-E* "fell out of the sky" for Stanton,<sup id="cite_ref-screenwriting_28-2"><a href="https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/WALL-E#cite_note-screenwriting-28">[28]</a></sup> he had originally wanted aliens to plant EVE to explore Earth and the rest of the film was different. When WALL-E comes to the *Axiom*, he incites a *[Spartacus](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Spartacus_(film) "Spartacus (film)")*\-style rebellion by the robots against the remnants of the human race, which were cruel alien Gels (completely devolved, gelatinous, boneless, legless, see-through, green creatures that resemble [Jell-O](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Jell-O "Jell-O")). James Hicks, a physiologist, mentioned to Stanton the concept of [atrophy](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Atrophy "Atrophy") and the effects prolonged [weightlessness](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Weightlessness "Weightlessness") would have on humans living in space for an inordinately extended time period.<sup id="cite_ref-notes_21-2"><a href="https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/WALL-E#cite_note-notes-21">[21]</a></sup><sup id="cite_ref-35"><a href="https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/WALL-E#cite_note-35">[35]</a></sup><sup id="cite_ref-36"><a href="https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/WALL-E#cite_note-36">[36]</a></sup> Therefore, this was the inspiration of the humans degenerating into the alien Gels,<sup id="cite_ref-deleted_37-0"><a href="https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/WALL-E#cite_note-deleted-37">[37]</a></sup> and their ancestry would have been revealed in a *[Planet of the Apes](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Planet_of_the_Apes_(1968_film) "Planet of the Apes (1968 film)")*\-style ending.<sup id="cite_ref-newsarama2_38-0"><a href="https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/WALL-E#cite_note-newsarama2-38">[38]</a></sup> The Gels also spoke a made-up gibberish language, but Stanton scrapped this idea because he thought it would be too complicated for the audience to understand and they could easily be driven off from the storyline.<sup id="cite_ref-evolution_39-0"><a href="https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/WALL-E#cite_note-evolution-39">[39]</a></sup> The Gels had a royal family, who host a dance in a castle on a lake in the back of the ship, and the *Axiom* curled up into a ball when returning to Earth in this incarnation of the story.<sup id="cite_ref-evolution_39-1"><a href="https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/WALL-E#cite_note-evolution-39">[39]</a></sup> Stanton decided this was too bizarre and unengaging, and conceived humanity as "big babies".<sup id="cite_ref-newsarama2_38-1"><a href="https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/WALL-E#cite_note-newsarama2-38">[38]</a></sup> Stanton developed the metaphorical theme of the humans learning to stand again and "grow\[ing\] up",<sup id="cite_ref-newsarama2_38-2"><a href="https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/WALL-E#cite_note-newsarama2-38">[38]</a></sup><sup id="cite_ref-40"><a href="https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/WALL-E#cite_note-40">[40]</a></sup> wanting WALL-E and EVE's relationship to inspire humanity because he felt few films explore how [utopian](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Utopia "Utopia") societies come to exist.<sup id="cite_ref-aicn_41-0"><a href="https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/WALL-E#cite_note-aicn-41">[41]</a></sup> The process of depicting the descendants of humanity as the way they appear in the movie was slow. Stanton first decided to put a nose and ears on the Gels so the audience could recognize them. Eventually, fingers, legs, clothes, and other characteristics were added until they arrived at the concept of being fetus-like to allow the audience to see themselves in the characters.<sup id="cite_ref-evolution_39-2"><a href="https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/WALL-E#cite_note-evolution-39">[39]</a></sup>

In a later version of the film, AUTO comes to the docking bay to retrieve EVE's plant. The film would have its first cutaway to the captain, but Stanton moved that as he found it too early to begin moving away from WALL·E's point-of-view. As an homage to *[Get Smart](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Get_Smart "Get Smart")*,<sup id="cite_ref-comingsoon_42-0"><a href="https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/WALL-E#cite_note-comingsoon-42">[42]</a></sup> AUTO takes the plant and goes into the bowels of the ship into a room resembling a brain where he watches videos of Buy n Large's scheme to clean up the Earth falling apart through the years. Stanton removed this to keep some mystery as to why the plant is taken from EVE. The captain appears to be unintelligent, but Stanton wanted him to just be unchallenged; otherwise he would have not been sympathetic.<sup id="cite_ref-deleted_37-1"><a href="https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/WALL-E#cite_note-deleted-37">[37]</a></sup> One example of how unintelligent the captain was depicted initially is that he was seen to wear his hat upside-down, only to fix it before he challenges AUTO. In the finished film, he merely wears it casually atop his head, tightening it when he really takes command of the *Axiom*.<sup id="cite_ref-evolution_39-3"><a href="https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/WALL-E#cite_note-evolution-39">[39]</a></sup>

Stanton also moved the moment where WALL-E reveals his plant (which he had snatched from the self-destructing escape pod) from producing it from a closet to immediately after his escape, as it made EVE happier and gave them stronger motivation to dance around the ship. Originally, EVE would have been electrocuted by AUTO, and then be quickly saved from ejection at the hands of the *Waste Allocation Load Lifter: Axiom-class* (WALL·A) robots, by WALL-E. He would have then revived her by replacing her power unit with a cigarette lighter he brought from Earth. Stanton reversed this following a 2007 test screening, as he wanted to show EVE replacing her directive of bringing the plant to the captain with repairing WALL-E, and it made WALL-E even more heroic if he held the holo-detector open despite being badly hurt.<sup id="cite_ref-deleted_37-2"><a href="https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/WALL-E#cite_note-deleted-37">[37]</a></sup> The idea of WALL-E losing his memory and personality came after the braintrust team abandoned a scene where EVE had been shot in her heart battery and WALL-E gave her his own battery instead.<sup id="cite_ref-43"><a href="https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/WALL-E#cite_note-43">[43]</a></sup> Stanton felt half the audience at the screening believed the humans would be unable to cope with living on Earth and would have died out after the film's end. [Jim Capobianco](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Jim_Capobianco "Jim Capobianco"), director of the *[Ratatouille](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ratatouille_(film) "Ratatouille (film)")* short film *[Your Friend the Rat](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Your_Friend_the_Rat "Your Friend the Rat")*, created an end credits animation that continued the story—and stylized in different artistic movements throughout history—to clarify an optimistic tone.<sup id="cite_ref-44"><a href="https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/WALL-E#cite_note-44">[44]</a></sup>

### Design

*WALL-E* was the most complex Pixar production since *Monsters, Inc.* because of the world and the history that had to be conveyed.<sup id="cite_ref-awn_24-1"><a href="https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/WALL-E#cite_note-awn-24">[24]</a></sup> Whereas most Pixar films have up to 75,000 [storyboards](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Storyboard "Storyboard"), *WALL-E* required 125,000.<sup id="cite_ref-45"><a href="https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/WALL-E#cite_note-45">[45]</a></sup> [Production designer](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Production_designer "Production designer") [Ralph Eggleston](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ralph_Eggleston "Ralph Eggleston") wanted the lighting of the first act on Earth to be romantic, and that of the second act on the *Axiom* to be cold and sterile. During the third act, the romantic lighting is slowly introduced into the *Axiom* environment.<sup id="cite_ref-notes_21-3"><a href="https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/WALL-E#cite_note-notes-21">[21]</a></sup> Pixar studied [Chernobyl](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Chernobyl "Chernobyl") and the city of [Sofia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sofia "Sofia") to create the ruined world; art director Anthony Christov was from [Bulgaria](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bulgaria "Bulgaria") and recalled Sofia used to have problems storing its garbage.<sup id="cite_ref-46"><a href="https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/WALL-E#cite_note-46">[46]</a></sup><sup id="cite_ref-47"><a href="https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/WALL-E#cite_note-47">[47]</a></sup> Eggleston bleached out the whites on Earth to make WALL-E feel vulnerable. The overexposed light makes the location look more vast. Because of the haziness, the cubes making up the towers of garbage had to be large, otherwise they would have lost shape (in turn, this helped save rendering time). The dull tans of Earth subtly become soft pinks and blues when EVE arrives. When WALL-E shows EVE all his collected items, all the lights he has collected light up to give an inviting atmosphere, like a [Christmas tree](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Christmas_tree "Christmas tree"). Eggleston tried to avoid the colors yellow and green so WALL-E—who was made yellow to emulate a tractor—would not blend into the deserted Earth, and to make the plant more prominent.<sup id="cite_ref-eggleston_48-0"><a href="https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/WALL-E#cite_note-eggleston-48">[48]</a></sup>

[![WALL-E holding a bra](https://upload.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/en/thumb/2/20/Wall-E_lighting.png/220px-Wall-E_lighting.png)](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/File:Wall-E_lighting.png)

[WALL·E](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/WALL-E_(character) "WALL-E (character)") finds a bra. [Roger Deakins](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Roger_Deakins "Roger Deakins") and [Dennis Muren](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dennis_Muren "Dennis Muren") were consulted on realistic lighting including backgrounds that are less focused than foregrounds.

Stanton also wanted the lighting to look realistic and evoke the science fiction films of his youth. He thought that Pixar captured the physics of being underwater with *[Finding Nemo](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Finding_Nemo "Finding Nemo")* and so for *WALL·E*, he wanted to push that for air. While rewatching some of his favorite science fiction films, he realized that Pixar's other movies had lacked the look of [70 mm film](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/70_mm_film "70 mm film") and its barrel [distortion](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Distortion_(optics) "Distortion (optics)"), [lens flare](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lens_flare "Lens flare"), and [racking focus](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Racking_focus "Racking focus").<sup id="cite_ref-awn_24-2"><a href="https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/WALL-E#cite_note-awn-24">[24]</a></sup> Producer Jim Morris invited [Roger Deakins](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Roger_Deakins "Roger Deakins") and [Dennis Muren](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dennis_Muren "Dennis Muren") to advise on lighting and atmosphere. Muren spent several months with Pixar, while Deakins hosted one talk and was requested to stay on for another two weeks. Stanton said Muren's experience came from integrating computer animation into live-action settings, while Deakins helped them understand not to overly complicate their camerawork and lighting.<sup id="cite_ref-aicn_41-1"><a href="https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/WALL-E#cite_note-aicn-41">[41]</a></sup> 1970s [Panavision cameras](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Panavision_cameras "Panavision cameras") were used to help the animators understand and replicate handheld imperfections like unfocused backgrounds in digital environments.<sup id="cite_ref-notes_21-4"><a href="https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/WALL-E#cite_note-notes-21">[21]</a></sup> The first lighting test included building a three-dimensional replica of WALL-E, filming it with a 70 mm camera, and then trying to replicate that in the computer.<sup id="cite_ref-ign_49-0"><a href="https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/WALL-E#cite_note-ign-49">[49]</a></sup> Stanton cited the shallow lens work of [Gus Van Sant](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Gus_Van_Sant "Gus Van Sant")'s films as an influence, as it created intimacy in each close-up. Stanton chose angles for the virtual cameras that a live-action filmmaker would choose if filming on a set.<sup id="cite_ref-screenwriting_28-3"><a href="https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/WALL-E#cite_note-screenwriting-28">[28]</a></sup>

Stanton wanted the *Axiom*'s interior to resemble Shanghai and [Dubai](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dubai "Dubai").<sup id="cite_ref-awn_24-3"><a href="https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/WALL-E#cite_note-awn-24">[24]</a></sup> Eggleston studied 1960s NASA paintings and the original concept art for [Tomorrowland](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tomorrowland_(Disney_Parks) "Tomorrowland (Disney Parks)") for the *Axiom*, to reflect that era's sense of optimism.<sup id="cite_ref-notes_21-5"><a href="https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/WALL-E#cite_note-notes-21">[21]</a></sup> Stanton remarked "We are all probably very similar in our backgrounds here \[at Pixar\] in that we all miss the Tomorrowland that was promised us from the heyday of [Disneyland](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Disneyland "Disneyland")," and wanted a "[jet pack](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Jet_pack "Jet pack")" feel.<sup id="cite_ref-awn_24-4"><a href="https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/WALL-E#cite_note-awn-24">[24]</a></sup> Pixar also studied the [Disney Cruise Line](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Disney_Cruise_Line "Disney Cruise Line") and visited [Las Vegas](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Las_Vegas "Las Vegas"), which was helpful in understanding artificial lighting.<sup id="cite_ref-notes_21-6"><a href="https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/WALL-E#cite_note-notes-21">[21]</a></sup> Eggleston based his *Axiom* designs on the futuristic architecture of [Santiago Calatrava](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Santiago_Calatrava "Santiago Calatrava"). Eggleston divided the inside of the ship into three sections; the rear's economy class has a basic gray concrete texture with graphics keeping to the red, blue, and white of the BnL logo. The coach class with living/shopping spaces has "S" shapes as people are always looking for "what's around the corner". Stanton intended to have many colorful signs, but he realized this would overwhelm the audience and went with Eggleston's original idea of a small number of larger signs. The premier class is a large [Zen](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Zen "Zen")\-like spa with colors limited to turquoise, cream, and tan, and leads on to the captain's warm carpeted and wooded quarters and the sleek dark bridge.<sup id="cite_ref-eggleston_48-1"><a href="https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/WALL-E#cite_note-eggleston-48">[48]</a></sup> In keeping with the artificial *Axiom*, camera movements were modeled after those of the [steadicam](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Steadicam "Steadicam").<sup id="cite_ref-50"><a href="https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/WALL-E#cite_note-50">[50]</a></sup>

The use of live action was a stepping stone for Pixar, as Stanton was planning to make *[John Carter of Mars](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/John_Carter_(film) "John Carter (film)")* his next project.<sup id="cite_ref-awn_24-5"><a href="https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/WALL-E#cite_note-awn-24">[24]</a></sup> Storyboarder Derek Thompson noted introducing live action meant that they would make the rest of the film look even more realistic.<sup id="cite_ref-ripple_51-0"><a href="https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/WALL-E#cite_note-ripple-51">[51]</a></sup> Eggleston added that if the historical humans had been animated and slightly caricaturized, the audience then would not have been able to recognize how serious their devolution was.<sup id="cite_ref-eggleston_48-2"><a href="https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/WALL-E#cite_note-eggleston-48">[48]</a></sup> Stanton cast [Fred Willard](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Fred_Willard "Fred Willard") as the historical Buy n Large CEO because "\[h\]e's the most friendly and insincere car salesman I could think of."<sup id="cite_ref-newsarama2_38-3"><a href="https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/WALL-E#cite_note-newsarama2-38">[38]</a></sup> The CEO says "[stay the course](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Stay_the_course "Stay the course")", which Stanton used because he thought it was funny.<sup id="cite_ref-moviesonline_52-0"><a href="https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/WALL-E#cite_note-moviesonline-52">[52]</a></sup> [Industrial Light & Magic](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Industrial_Light_%26_Magic "Industrial Light & Magic") did the visual effects for these shots.<sup id="cite_ref-notes_21-7"><a href="https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/WALL-E#cite_note-notes-21">[21]</a></sup>

### Animation

*WALL·E* went undeveloped during the 1990s partly because Stanton and Pixar were not confident enough yet to have a feature-length film with a main character that behaved like [Luxo Jr.](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Luxo_Jr._(character) "Luxo Jr. (character)"), the [Pixar lamp](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Balanced-arm_lamp "Balanced-arm lamp") or [R2-D2](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/R2-D2 "R2-D2").<sup id="cite_ref-avclub_25-2"><a href="https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/WALL-E#cite_note-avclub-25">[25]</a></sup> Stanton explained there are two types of robots in cinema: "human\[s\] with metal skin", like the [Tin Man](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tin_Woodman "Tin Woodman"), or "machine\[s\] with function" like Luxo and R2. He found the latter idea "powerful" because it allowed the audience to project personalities onto the characters, as they do with babies and pets: "You're compelled ... you almost can't stop yourself from finishing the sentence 'Oh, I think it likes me! I think it's hungry! I think it wants to go for a walk!'"<sup id="cite_ref-newsarama1_53-0"><a href="https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/WALL-E#cite_note-newsarama1-53">[53]</a></sup> He added, "We wanted the audience to believe they were witnessing a machine that has come to life."<sup id="cite_ref-notes_21-8"><a href="https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/WALL-E#cite_note-notes-21">[21]</a></sup> The animators visited recycling stations to study machinery, and also met robot designers, visited [NASA](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/NASA "NASA")'s [Jet Propulsion Laboratory](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Jet_Propulsion_Laboratory "Jet Propulsion Laboratory") to study robots, watched a recording of a [Mars rover](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mars_rover "Mars rover"),<sup id="cite_ref-usatoday_32-1"><a href="https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/WALL-E#cite_note-usatoday-32">[32]</a></sup> and borrowed a [bomb detecting robot](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Remote_control_vehicle#Military_and_law_enforcement "Remote control vehicle") from the [San Francisco Police Department](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/San_Francisco_Police_Department "San Francisco Police Department"). Simplicity was preferred in their performances as giving them too many movements would make them feel human.<sup id="cite_ref-notes_21-9"><a href="https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/WALL-E#cite_note-notes-21">[21]</a></sup>

Stanton wanted WALL·E to be a box and EVE to be like an egg.<sup id="cite_ref-duo_54-0"><a href="https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/WALL-E#cite_note-duo-54">[54]</a></sup> WALL·E's eyes were inspired by a pair of [binoculars](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Binoculars "Binoculars") Stanton was given when watching the [Oakland Athletics](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Oakland_Athletics "Oakland Athletics") play against the [Boston Red Sox](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Boston_Red_Sox "Boston Red Sox"). He "missed the entire inning" because he was distracted by them.<sup id="cite_ref-planet_55-0"><a href="https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/WALL-E#cite_note-planet-55">[55]</a></sup> The director was reminded of [Buster Keaton](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Buster_Keaton "Buster Keaton") and decided the robot would not need a nose or mouth.<sup id="cite_ref-nydn_56-0"><a href="https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/WALL-E#cite_note-nydn-56">[56]</a></sup> Stanton added a [zoom lens](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Zoom_lens "Zoom lens") to make WALL-E more sympathetic.<sup id="cite_ref-nydn_56-1"><a href="https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/WALL-E#cite_note-nydn-56">[56]</a></sup> [Ralph Eggleston](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ralph_Eggleston "Ralph Eggleston") noted this feature gave the animators more to work with and gave the robot a childlike quality.<sup id="cite_ref-eggleston_48-3"><a href="https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/WALL-E#cite_note-eggleston-48">[48]</a></sup> Pixar's studies of trash [compactors](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Compactor "Compactor") during their visits to recycling stations inspired his body.<sup id="cite_ref-notes_21-10"><a href="https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/WALL-E#cite_note-notes-21">[21]</a></sup> His tank treads were inspired by a wheelchair someone had developed that used treads instead of wheels.<sup id="cite_ref-duo_54-1"><a href="https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/WALL-E#cite_note-duo-54">[54]</a></sup> The animators wanted him to have elbows, but realized this was unrealistic because he is only designed to pull garbage into his body.<sup id="cite_ref-notes_21-11"><a href="https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/WALL-E#cite_note-notes-21">[21]</a></sup> His arms also looked flimsy when they did a test of him waving.<sup id="cite_ref-duo_54-2"><a href="https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/WALL-E#cite_note-duo-54">[54]</a></sup> Animation director Angus MacLane suggested they attach his arms to a track on the sides of his body to move them around, based on the [inkjet printers](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Inkjet_printer "Inkjet printer") his father designed. This arm design contributed to creating the character's posture, so if they wanted him to be nervous, they would lower them.<sup id="cite_ref-better_57-0"><a href="https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/WALL-E#cite_note-better-57">[57]</a></sup> Stanton was unaware of the similarities between WALL-E and Johnny 5 from *[Short Circuit](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Short_Circuit_(1986_film) "Short Circuit (1986 film)")* until others pointed it out to him.<sup id="cite_ref-avclub_25-3"><a href="https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/WALL-E#cite_note-avclub-25">[25]</a></sup>

Stanton wanted EVE to be at the higher end of technology, and asked [iPod](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/IPod "IPod") designer [Jonathan Ive](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Jonathan_Ive "Jonathan Ive") to inspect her design. He was very impressed.<sup id="cite_ref-awn_24-6"><a href="https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/WALL-E#cite_note-awn-24">[24]</a></sup> Her eyes are modelled on [Lite-Brite](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lite-Brite "Lite-Brite") toys,<sup id="cite_ref-nydn_56-2"><a href="https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/WALL-E#cite_note-nydn-56">[56]</a></sup> but Pixar chose not to make them overly expressive as it would be too easy to have her eyes turn into hearts to express love or something similar.<sup id="cite_ref-duo_54-3"><a href="https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/WALL-E#cite_note-duo-54">[54]</a></sup> Her limited design meant the animators had to treat her like a drawing, relying on posing her body to express emotion.<sup id="cite_ref-notes_21-12"><a href="https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/WALL-E#cite_note-notes-21">[21]</a></sup> They also found her similar to a [manatee](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Manatee "Manatee") or a [narwhal](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Narwhal "Narwhal") because her floating body resembled an underwater creature.<sup id="cite_ref-duo_54-4"><a href="https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/WALL-E#cite_note-duo-54">[54]</a></sup> Auto was a conscious homage to [HAL 9000](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/HAL_9000 "HAL 9000") from *[2001: A Space Odyssey](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/2001:_A_Space_Odyssey_(film) "2001: A Space Odyssey (film)")*,<sup id="cite_ref-avclub_25-4"><a href="https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/WALL-E#cite_note-avclub-25">[25]</a></sup> and the usage of [*Also sprach Zarathustra*](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Also_sprach_Zarathustra_(Strauss) "Also sprach Zarathustra (Strauss)") for the showdown between Captain McCrea and Auto furthers that.<sup id="cite_ref-58"><a href="https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/WALL-E#cite_note-58">[58]</a></sup> The manner in which he hangs from a wall or ceiling gives him a threatening feel, like a spider.<sup id="cite_ref-robot_59-0"><a href="https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/WALL-E#cite_note-robot-59">[59]</a></sup> Originally, Auto was designed entirely differently, resembling EVE, but masculine and authoritative and SECUR-T was also a more aggressive patrol steward robot.<sup id="cite_ref-deleted_37-3"><a href="https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/WALL-E#cite_note-deleted-37">[37]</a></sup> The majority of the robot cast were formed with the Build-a-bot program, where different heads, arms and treads were combined in over a hundred variations.<sup id="cite_ref-notes_21-13"><a href="https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/WALL-E#cite_note-notes-21">[21]</a></sup> The humans were modelled on sea lions due to their [blubbery](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Blubber "Blubber") bodies,<sup id="cite_ref-eggleston_48-4"><a href="https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/WALL-E#cite_note-eggleston-48">[48]</a></sup> as well as babies. The filmmakers noticed baby fat is a lot tighter than adult fat and copied that texture for the film's humans.<sup id="cite_ref-60"><a href="https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/WALL-E#cite_note-60">[60]</a></sup>

To animate their robots, the film's story crew and animation crew watched a Keaton and a [Charlie Chaplin](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Charlie_Chaplin "Charlie Chaplin") film every day for almost a year, and occasionally a [Harold Lloyd](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Harold_Lloyd "Harold Lloyd") picture.<sup id="cite_ref-avclub_25-5"><a href="https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/WALL-E#cite_note-avclub-25">[25]</a></sup> Afterwards, the filmmakers knew all emotions could be conveyed silently. Stanton cited Keaton's "great stone face" as giving them perseverance in animating a character with an unchanging expression.<sup id="cite_ref-nydn_56-3"><a href="https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/WALL-E#cite_note-nydn-56">[56]</a></sup> As he rewatched these, Stanton felt that filmmakers—since the advent of sound—relied on dialogue too much to convey exposition.<sup id="cite_ref-avclub_25-6"><a href="https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/WALL-E#cite_note-avclub-25">[25]</a></sup> The filmmakers dubbed the cockroach WALL-E keeps as a pet "Hal", in reference to silent film producer [Hal Roach](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hal_Roach "Hal Roach") (as well as being an additional reference to HAL 9000).<sup id="cite_ref-notes_21-14"><a href="https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/WALL-E#cite_note-notes-21">[21]</a></sup> They also watched *2001: A Space Odyssey*, *[The Black Stallion](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/The_Black_Stallion_(film) "The Black Stallion (film)")* and *[Never Cry Wolf](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Never_Cry_Wolf_(film) "Never Cry Wolf (film)")*, films that had sound but were not reliant on dialogue.<sup id="cite_ref-ripple_51-1"><a href="https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/WALL-E#cite_note-ripple-51">[51]</a></sup> Stanton acknowledged *[Silent Running](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Silent_Running "Silent Running")* as an influence because its silent robots were a forerunner to the likes of R2-D2,<sup id="cite_ref-aicn_41-2"><a href="https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/WALL-E#cite_note-aicn-41">[41]</a></sup> and that the "hopeless romantic" [Woody Allen](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Woody_Allen "Woody Allen") also inspired WALL-E.<sup id="cite_ref-total_29-1"><a href="https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/WALL-E#cite_note-total-29">[29]</a></sup>

### Sound

Producer Jim Morris recommended [Ben Burtt](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ben_Burtt "Ben Burtt") as [sound designer](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sound_design "Sound design") for *WALL-E* because Stanton kept using [R2-D2](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/R2-D2 "R2-D2") as the benchmark for the robots.<sup id="cite_ref-comingsoon_42-1"><a href="https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/WALL-E#cite_note-comingsoon-42">[42]</a></sup> Burtt had completed *[Star Wars: Episode III – Revenge of the Sith](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Star_Wars:_Episode_III_%E2%80%93_Revenge_of_the_Sith "Star Wars: Episode III – Revenge of the Sith")* and told his wife he would no longer work on films with robots, but found *WALL-E* and its substitution of voices with sound "fresh and exciting".<sup id="cite_ref-notes_21-15"><a href="https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/WALL-E#cite_note-notes-21">[21]</a></sup> He recorded 2,500 sounds for the film, which was twice the average number for a *[Star Wars](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Star_Wars "Star Wars")* film,<sup id="cite_ref-usatoday_32-2"><a href="https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/WALL-E#cite_note-usatoday-32">[32]</a></sup> and a record in his career.<sup id="cite_ref-notes_21-16"><a href="https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/WALL-E#cite_note-notes-21">[21]</a></sup> Burtt began work in 2005,<sup id="cite_ref-61"><a href="https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/WALL-E#cite_note-61">[61]</a></sup> and experimented with filtering his voice for two years.<sup id="cite_ref-62"><a href="https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/WALL-E#cite_note-62">[62]</a></sup> Burtt described the robot voices as "like a [toddler](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Toddler "Toddler") ... [universal language](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Universal_language "Universal language") of [intonation](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Intonation_(linguistics) "Intonation (linguistics)"). 'Oh', 'Hm?', 'Huh!', you know?"<sup id="cite_ref-63"><a href="https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/WALL-E#cite_note-63">[63]</a></sup>

During production Burtt had the opportunity to look at the items used by [Jimmy MacDonald](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Jimmy_MacDonald_(sound_effects_artist) "Jimmy MacDonald (sound effects artist)"), Disney's in-house sound designer for many of their classic films. Burtt used many of MacDonald's items on *WALL-E*. Because Burtt was not simply adding sound effects in post-production, the animators were always evaluating his new creations and ideas, which Burtt found an unusual experience.<sup id="cite_ref-sound_64-0"><a href="https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/WALL-E#cite_note-sound-64">[64]</a></sup> He worked in sync with the animators, returning their animation after adding the sounds to give them more ideas.<sup id="cite_ref-notes_21-17"><a href="https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/WALL-E#cite_note-notes-21">[21]</a></sup> Burtt would choose scientifically accurate sounds for each character, but if he could not find one that worked, he would choose a dramatic and unrealistic noise.<sup id="cite_ref-sound_64-1"><a href="https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/WALL-E#cite_note-sound-64">[64]</a></sup> Burtt would find hundreds of sounds by looking at concept art of characters, before he and Stanton pared it down to a distinct few for each robot.<sup id="cite_ref-awn_24-7"><a href="https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/WALL-E#cite_note-awn-24">[24]</a></sup>

Burtt saw a hand-cranked electrical generator while watching *[Island in the Sky](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Island_in_the_Sky_(1953_film) "Island in the Sky (1953 film)")*, and bought an identical, unpacked device from 1950 on [eBay](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/EBay "EBay") to use for WALL·E moving around.<sup id="cite_ref-65"><a href="https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/WALL-E#cite_note-65">[65]</a></sup> Burtt also used an [automobile self-starter](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Starter_(engine) "Starter (engine)") for when WALL·E goes fast,<sup id="cite_ref-sound_64-2"><a href="https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/WALL-E#cite_note-sound-64">[64]</a></sup> and the sound of cars being wrecked at a [demolition derby](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Demolition_derby "Demolition derby") provided for WALL·E's compressing trash in his body.<sup id="cite_ref-shot_66-0"><a href="https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/WALL-E#cite_note-shot-66">[66]</a></sup> The [Macintosh](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Macintosh "Macintosh") computer [chime](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Chime_(Macintosh) "Chime (Macintosh)") was used to signify when WALL-E has fully recharged his battery. For EVE, Burtt wanted her humming to have a musical quality.<sup id="cite_ref-sound_64-3"><a href="https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/WALL-E#cite_note-sound-64">[64]</a></sup> Burtt was only able to provide neutral or masculine voices, so Pixar employee Elissa Knight was asked to provide her voice for Burtt to electronically modify. Stanton deemed the sound effect good enough to properly cast her in the role.<sup id="cite_ref-moviesonline_52-1"><a href="https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/WALL-E#cite_note-moviesonline-52">[52]</a></sup> Burtt recorded a flying 10-foot-long (3.0 m) radio-controlled jet plane for EVE's flying,<sup id="cite_ref-notes_21-18"><a href="https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/WALL-E#cite_note-notes-21">[21]</a></sup> and for her plasma cannon, Burtt hit a [slinky](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Slinky "Slinky") hung from a ladder with a [timpani](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Timpani "Timpani") stick. He described it as a "cousin" to the [blaster](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Blaster_(Star_Wars) "Blaster (Star Wars)") noise from *Star Wars*.<sup id="cite_ref-slinky_67-0"><a href="https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/WALL-E#cite_note-slinky-67">[67]</a></sup>

[MacInTalk](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/PlainTalk "PlainTalk") was used because Stanton "wanted Auto to be the epitome of a robot, cold, zeros & ones, calculating, and soulless \[and\] [Stephen Hawking](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Stephen_Hawking "Stephen Hawking")'s kind of voice I thought was perfect."<sup id="cite_ref-aicn_41-3"><a href="https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/WALL-E#cite_note-aicn-41">[41]</a></sup> Additional sounds for the character were meant to give him a clockwork feel, to show he is always thinking and calculating.<sup id="cite_ref-sound_64-4"><a href="https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/WALL-E#cite_note-sound-64">[64]</a></sup>

Burtt had visited [Niagara Falls](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Niagara_Falls "Niagara Falls") in 1987 and used his recordings from his trip for the sounds of wind,<sup id="cite_ref-shot_66-1"><a href="https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/WALL-E#cite_note-shot-66">[66]</a></sup> and ran around a hall with a [canvas](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Canvas "Canvas") bag up to record the sandstorm.<sup id="cite_ref-notes_21-19"><a href="https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/WALL-E#cite_note-notes-21">[21]</a></sup> For the scene where WALL-E flees from falling shopping carts, Burtt and his daughter went to a supermarket and placed a recorder in their cart. They crashed it around the parking lot and then let it tumble down a hill.<sup id="cite_ref-ears_68-0"><a href="https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/WALL-E#cite_note-ears-68">[68]</a></sup> To create Hal (WALL-E's pet cockroach)'s skittering, he recorded the clicking caused by taking apart and reassembling [handcuffs](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Handcuffs "Handcuffs").<sup id="cite_ref-notes_21-20"><a href="https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/WALL-E#cite_note-notes-21">[21]</a></sup>

### Music

[Thomas Newman](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Thomas_Newman "Thomas Newman") recollaborated with Stanton on *WALL-E* since the two got along well on *Finding Nemo*, which gave Newman the Annie Award for Best Music in an Animated Feature. He began writing the score in 2005, in the hope that starting this task early would make him more involved with the finished film. But, Newman remarked that animation is so dependent on scheduling he should have begun work earlier on when Stanton and Reardon were writing the script. EVE's theme was arranged for the first time in October 2007. Her theme when played as she first flies around Earth originally used more orchestral elements, and Newman was encouraged to make it sound more feminine.<sup id="cite_ref-69"><a href="https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/WALL-E#cite_note-69">[69]</a></sup> Newman said Stanton had thought up many ideas for how he wanted the music to sound, and he generally followed them as he found scoring a partially silent film difficult. Stanton wanted the whole score to be orchestral, but Newman felt limited by this idea especially in scenes aboard the *Axiom*, and used electronics too.<sup id="cite_ref-70"><a href="https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/WALL-E#cite_note-70">[70]</a></sup>

[![WALL·E watching a clip from Hello, Dolly!](https://upload.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/en/thumb/5/54/WALL-E-Only-Takes-a-Moment.jpg/220px-WALL-E-Only-Takes-a-Moment.jpg)](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/File:WALL-E-Only-Takes-a-Moment.jpg)

A live-action clip of the song "It Only Takes a Moment" from *[Hello, Dolly!](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hello,_Dolly!_(film) "Hello, Dolly! (film)")*, which inspires WALL-E to hold hands with EVE

Stanton originally wanted to juxtapose the opening shots of space with 1930s French [swing music](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Swing_music "Swing music"), but changed his mind after seeing *[The Triplets of Belleville](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/The_Triplets_of_Belleville "The Triplets of Belleville")* (2003), not wanting to appear as if he were copying it.<sup id="cite_ref-EW_71-0"><a href="https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/WALL-E#cite_note-EW-71">[71]</a></sup> Stanton then thought about the song "Put On Your Sunday Clothes" from *[Hello, Dolly!](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hello,_Dolly!_(film) "Hello, Dolly! (film)")*, since he had portrayed the sidekick Barnaby Tucker in a 1980 high school production.<sup id="cite_ref-72"><a href="https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/WALL-E#cite_note-72">[72]</a></sup> Stanton found that the song was about two naive young men looking for love, which was similar to WALL-E's own hope for companionship. [Jim Reardon](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Jim_Reardon "Jim Reardon"), storyboard supervisor for the film, suggested WALL-E find the film on video, and Stanton included "It Only Takes a Moment" and the clip of the actors holding hands, because he wanted a visual way to show how WALL-E understands love and conveys it to EVE. *Hello, Dolly!* composer [Jerry Herman](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Jerry_Herman "Jerry Herman") allowed the songs to be used without knowing what for; when he saw the film, he found its incorporation into the story "genius".<sup id="cite_ref-EW_71-1"><a href="https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/WALL-E#cite_note-EW-71">[71]</a></sup> Coincidentally, Newman's uncle [Lionel](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lionel_Newman "Lionel Newman") worked on *Hello, Dolly!*<sup id="cite_ref-notes_21-21"><a href="https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/WALL-E#cite_note-notes-21">[21]</a></sup>

Newman travelled to London to compose the end credits song "[Down to Earth](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Down_to_Earth_(Peter_Gabriel_song) "Down to Earth (Peter Gabriel song)")" with [Peter Gabriel](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Peter_Gabriel "Peter Gabriel"), who was one of Stanton's favorite musicians. Afterwards, Newman rescored some of the film to include the song's composition, so it would not sound intrusive when played.<sup id="cite_ref-notes_21-22"><a href="https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/WALL-E#cite_note-notes-21">[21]</a></sup> [Louis Armstrong](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Louis_Armstrong "Louis Armstrong")'s rendition of "[La Vie en rose](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/La_Vie_en_rose "La Vie en rose")" was used for a montage where WALL-E attempts to impress EVE on Earth. The script also specified using [Bing Crosby](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bing_Crosby "Bing Crosby")'s "[Stardust](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Stardust_(1927_song) "Stardust (1927 song)")" for when the two robots dance around the *Axiom*,<sup id="cite_ref-screenplay_23-1"><a href="https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/WALL-E#cite_note-screenplay-23">[23]</a></sup> but Newman asked if he could score the scene himself. A similar switch occurred for the sequence in which WALL-E attempts to wake EVE up through various means; originally, the montage would play with the instrumental version of "[Raindrops Keep Fallin' on My Head](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Raindrops_Keep_Fallin%27_on_My_Head "Raindrops Keep Fallin' on My Head")", but Newman wanted to challenge himself and scored an original piece for the sequence.

## Themes

The film is recognized as a [social criticism](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Social_criticism "Social criticism"). Katherine Ellison asserts that "Americans produce nearly 400 million tons of [solid waste](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Solid_waste "Solid waste") per year but recycle less than a third of it, according to a recent Columbia University study." [Landfills](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Landfill "Landfill") were filling up very rapidly worldwide, and predictions were made that the UK could run out of landfill space by 2017.<sup id="cite_ref-Ellison,_2008_74-0"><a href="https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/WALL-E#cite_note-Ellison,_2008-74">[74]</a></sup>

### Environment, waste, and nostalgia

In the DVD commentary, Stanton said that he has been asked if it was his intention to make a movie about consumerism. His answer was it was not; it was a way to answer the question of how would the Earth get to the state where one robot would be left to continue the cleanup by itself. Nevertheless, some critics have noted an incongruity between the perceived pro-environmental and anti-consumerist messaging of the film, and the environmental impacts in the production and merchandising of the film.<sup id="cite_ref-75"><a href="https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/WALL-E#cite_note-75">[75]</a></sup>

In "WALL-E: from environmental adaption to sentimental [nostalgia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Nostalgia "Nostalgia")," Robin Murray and Joseph Heumann explain the important theme of nostalgia in this film. Nostalgia is clearly represented by human artifacts, left behind, that WALL-E collects and cherishes, for example [Zippo](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Zippo "Zippo") lighters, hubcaps, and plastic sporks. These modern items that are used out of necessity are made sentimental through the lens of the bleak future of Earth. Nostalgia is also expressed through the musical score, as the film opens with a camera shot of outer space that slowly zooms into a waste filled Earth while playing "Put on Your Sunday Clothes", reflecting on simpler and happier times in human history. This film also expresses nostalgia through the longing of nature and the natural world, as it is the sight and feeling of soil, and the plant brought back to the space ship by EVE, that make the captain decide it is time for humans to move back to Earth. WALL-E expresses nostalgia also, by reflecting on romantic themes of older Disney and silent films.<sup id="cite_ref-Murray_and_Heumann,_2009_6-1"><a href="https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/WALL-E#cite_note-Murray_and_Heumann,_2009-6">[6]</a></sup>

Stanton describes the theme of the film as "irrational love defeats life's programming":<sup id="cite_ref-newsarama2_38-4"><a href="https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/WALL-E#cite_note-newsarama2-38">[38]</a></sup>

> I realized the point I was trying to push with these two programmed robots was the desire for them to try and figure out what the point of living was ... It took these really irrational acts of love to sort of discover them against how they were built ... I realized that that's a perfect metaphor for real life. We all fall into our habits, our routines and our ruts, consciously or unconsciously to avoid living. To avoid having to do the messy part. To avoid having relationships with other people or dealing with the person next to us. That's why we can all get on our cell phones and not have to deal with one another. I thought, 'That's a perfect amplification of the whole point of the movie.' I wanted to run with science in a way that would sort of logically project that.<sup id="cite_ref-newsarama2_38-5"><a href="https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/WALL-E#cite_note-newsarama2-38">[38]</a></sup>

### Technology

Stanton noted many commentators placed emphasis on the environmental aspect of humanity's complacency in the film, because "that disconnection is going to be the cause, indirectly, of anything that happens in life that's bad for humanity or the planet".<sup id="cite_ref-76"><a href="https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/WALL-E#cite_note-76">[76]</a></sup> Stanton said that by taking away effort to work, the robots also take away humanity's need to put effort into relationships.<sup id="cite_ref-robot_59-1"><a href="https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/WALL-E#cite_note-robot-59">[59]</a></sup> Christian journalist [Rod Dreher](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Rod_Dreher "Rod Dreher") saw technology as the complicated villain of the film. The humans' artificial lifestyle on the *Axiom* has separated them from nature, making them "slaves of both technology and their own base appetites, and have lost what makes them human". Dreher contrasted the hardworking, dirt covered WALL-E with the sleek clean robots on the ship. However, it is the humans and not the robots who make themselves redundant. Humans on the ship and on Earth have overused robots and the ultra-modern technology. During the end credits, humans and robots are shown working alongside each other to renew the Earth. "*WALL·E* is not a [Luddite](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Luddite "Luddite") film," he said. "It doesn't demonize technology. It only argues that technology is properly used to help humans cultivate their true nature—that it must be subordinate to human flourishing, and help move that along."<sup id="cite_ref-dreher_77-0"><a href="https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/WALL-E#cite_note-dreher-77">[77]</a></sup>

### Religion

[![](https://upload.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/thumb/f/fd/Dove_Sent_Forth_from_the_Ark.png/170px-Dove_Sent_Forth_from_the_Ark.png)](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/File:Dove_Sent_Forth_from_the_Ark.png)

The *Axiom* and EVE have been compared to the legend of [Noah's Ark](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Noah%27s_Ark "Noah's Ark") and the dove that [Noah](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Noah "Noah") sets forth from the Ark.

Stanton, who is a [Christian](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Christianity_in_the_United_States "Christianity in the United States"),<sup id="cite_ref-world_26-1"><a href="https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/WALL-E#cite_note-world-26">[26]</a></sup> named EVE after [the Biblical figure](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Eve "Eve") because WALL-E's loneliness reminded him of [Adam](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Adam "Adam"), before God created his wife.<sup id="cite_ref-78"><a href="https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/WALL-E#cite_note-78">[78]</a></sup> Dreher noted EVE's biblical namesake and saw her directive as an inversion of that story; EVE uses the plant to tell humanity to return to Earth and move away from the "[false god](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/False_god "False god")" of BnL and the lazy lifestyle it offers. In cohesion with the classical Christian viewpoint, WALL-E shows that work is what makes humans human. Whereas other sources would say that laziness and pleasure is paradise, WALL-E tries to show that that is not true. Dreher emphasized the false god parallels to BnL in a scene where a robot teaches infants "B is for Buy n Large, your very best friend", which he compared to modern corporations such as [McDonald's](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/McDonald%27s "McDonald's") creating [brand loyalty](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Brand_loyalty "Brand loyalty") in children.<sup id="cite_ref-dreher_77-1"><a href="https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/WALL-E#cite_note-dreher-77">[77]</a></sup> Megan Basham of *[World](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/World_(magazine) "World (magazine)")* magazine felt the film criticizes the pursuit of leisure, whereas WALL-E in his [stewardship](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Stewardship "Stewardship") learns to truly appreciate God's creation.<sup id="cite_ref-world_26-2"><a href="https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/WALL-E#cite_note-world-26">[26]</a></sup>

During writing, a Pixar employee noted to [Jim Reardon](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Jim_Reardon "Jim Reardon") that EVE was reminiscent of the [dove](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dove "Dove") with the [olive branch](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Olive_branch "Olive branch") from the story of [Noah's Ark](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Noah%27s_Ark "Noah's Ark"), and the story was reworked with EVE finding a plant to return humanity from its voyage.<sup id="cite_ref-79"><a href="https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/WALL-E#cite_note-79">[79]</a></sup> WALL-E himself has been compared to [Prometheus](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Prometheus "Prometheus"),<sup id="cite_ref-comingsoon_42-2"><a href="https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/WALL-E#cite_note-comingsoon-42">[42]</a></sup> [Sisyphus](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sisyphus "Sisyphus"),<sup id="cite_ref-dreher_77-2"><a href="https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/WALL-E#cite_note-dreher-77">[77]</a></sup> and [Butades](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Butades "Butades"): in an essay discussing WALL-E as representative of the artistic strive of Pixar itself, [Hrag Vartanian](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hrag_Vartanian "Hrag Vartanian") compared WALL-E to Butades in a scene where the robot expresses his love for EVE by making a sculpture of her from spare parts. "The [Ancient Greek](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ancient_Greece "Ancient Greece") tradition associates the birth of art with a [Corinthian](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Corinth "Corinth") maiden who longing to preserve her lover's shadow traces it on the wall before he departed for war. The myth reminds us that art was born out of longing and often means more for the creator than the muse. In the same way Stanton and his Pixar team have told us a deeply personal story about their love of cinema and their vision for animation through the prism of all types of relationships."<sup id="cite_ref-80"><a href="https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/WALL-E#cite_note-80">[80]</a></sup>

## Release

*WALL-E* premiered at the [Greek Theatre](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Greek_Theatre_(Los_Angeles) "Greek Theatre (Los Angeles)") in Los Angeles on June 23, 2008.<sup id="cite_ref-81"><a href="https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/WALL-E#cite_note-81">[81]</a></sup> Continuing a Pixar tradition, the film was paired with a [short film](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Short_subject "Short subject") for its theatrical release, *[Presto](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Presto_(film) "Presto (film)")*. The film is dedicated to [Justin Wright](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Justin_Wright "Justin Wright") (1981–2008), a Pixar animator who had worked on *[Ratatouille](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ratatouille_(film) "Ratatouille (film)")* and died of a heart attack before *WALL-E*'s release.<sup id="cite_ref-notes_21-23"><a href="https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/WALL-E#cite_note-notes-21">[21]</a></sup>

[Walt Disney Imagineering](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Walt_Disney_Imagineering "Walt Disney Imagineering") (WDI) built animatronic WALL-Es to promote the picture, which made appearances at [Disneyland Resort](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Disneyland_Resort "Disneyland Resort"),<sup id="cite_ref-82"><a href="https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/WALL-E#cite_note-82">[82]</a></sup> the [Franklin Institute](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Franklin_Institute "Franklin Institute"), the [Miami Science Museum](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Miami_Science_Museum "Miami Science Museum"), the [Seattle Center](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Seattle_Center "Seattle Center"), and the [Tokyo International Film Festival](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tokyo_International_Film_Festival "Tokyo International Film Festival").<sup id="cite_ref-jim_83-0"><a href="https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/WALL-E#cite_note-jim-83">[83]</a></sup> Due to safety concerns, the 318 kg robots were always strictly controlled and WDI always needed to know exactly what they were required to interact with. For this reason, they generally refused to have their puppets meet and greet children at the theme parks in case a WALL-E trod on a child's foot. Those who wanted to take a photograph with the character had to make do with a cardboard cutout.<sup id="cite_ref-84"><a href="https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/WALL-E#cite_note-84">[84]</a></sup>

The film was denied a theatrical release in [China](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/China "China").<sup id="cite_ref-85"><a href="https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/WALL-E#cite_note-85">[85]</a></sup>

In 2016, Jim Morris noted that the studio has no plans for a sequel, as they consider *WALL-E* a finished story with no need for continuation.<sup id="cite_ref-86"><a href="https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/WALL-E#cite_note-86">[86]</a></sup>

To promote the film, a website was created for Buy n Large, the featuring megacorporation. This website had fake press releases beginning in 2057, including "Wisconsin Mall Granted City Status," "BnL Announces Police Select." These fictional reports also quotes "analyst Richard Greenberg," which *[Multichannel News](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Multichannel_News "Multichannel News")* pointed said sounds like Richard Greenfield who has analysed Disney. Greenfield said that "I would be sincerely flattered if there is a connection between anything Pixar creates and our research work."<sup id="cite_ref-87"><a href="https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/WALL-E#cite_note-87">[87]</a></sup>

### Merchandise

Small quantities of merchandise were sold for *WALL-E*, as *[Cars](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cars_(film) "Cars (film)")* items were still popular, and many manufacturers were more interested in *[Speed Racer](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Speed_Racer_(film) "Speed Racer (film)")*, which was a successful product line despite the film's failure at the box office. Thinkway, which created the *WALL·E* toys, had previously made *[Toy Story](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Toy_Story "Toy Story")* dolls when other toy producers had not shown an interest.<sup id="cite_ref-jim_83-1"><a href="https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/WALL-E#cite_note-jim-83">[83]</a></sup> Among Thinkway's items were a WALL-E that danced when connected to a music player, a toy that could be taken apart and reassembled, and a groundbreaking remote control toy of him and EVE that had motion sensors that allowed them to interact with players.<sup id="cite_ref-penn_88-0"><a href="https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/WALL-E#cite_note-penn-88">[88]</a></sup> There were even plushies.<sup id="cite_ref-89"><a href="https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/WALL-E#cite_note-89">[89]</a></sup> The "Ultimate WALL-E" figures were not in stores until the film's home release in November 2008,<sup id="cite_ref-jim_83-2"><a href="https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/WALL-E#cite_note-jim-83">[83]</a></sup> at a retail price of almost $200, leading *The Patriot-News* to deem it an item for "hard-core fans and collectors only".<sup id="cite_ref-penn_88-1"><a href="https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/WALL-E#cite_note-penn-88">[88]</a></sup> On February 4, 2015, [Lego](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lego "Lego") announced that a WALL-E custom built by lead animator [Angus MacLane](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Angus_MacLane "Angus MacLane") was the latest design approved for mass production and release as part of [Lego Ideas](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lego_Ideas "Lego Ideas").<sup id="cite_ref-90"><a href="https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/WALL-E#cite_note-90">[90]</a></sup>

The film was released on [Blu-ray](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Blu-ray "Blu-ray") Disc and [DVD](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/DVD-Video "DVD-Video") by [Walt Disney Studios Home Entertainment](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Walt_Disney_Studios_Home_Entertainment "Walt Disney Studios Home Entertainment") on November 18, 2008. Various editions include the short film *[Presto](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Presto_(film) "Presto (film)")*, another short film *[BURN-E](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/BURN-E "BURN-E")*, the [Leslie Iwerks](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Leslie_Iwerks "Leslie Iwerks") documentary film *[The Pixar Story](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/The_Pixar_Story "The Pixar Story")*, shorts about the history of Buy n Large, behind-the-scenes special features, and a [digital copy](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Digital_copy "Digital copy") of the film that can be played through [iTunes](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/ITunes "ITunes") or [Windows Media Player](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Windows_Media_Player "Windows Media Player")\-compatible devices.<sup id="cite_ref-film_91-0"><a href="https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/WALL-E#cite_note-film-91">[91]</a></sup> This release sold 9,042,054 DVD units ($142,633,974) in total becoming the second-best-selling animated DVD among those released in 2008 in units sold (behind *[Kung Fu Panda](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Kung_Fu_Panda_(film) "Kung Fu Panda (film)")*), the best-selling animated feature in sales revenue, and the third-best-selling among all 2008 DVDs.<sup id="cite_ref-92"><a href="https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/WALL-E#cite_note-92">[92]</a></sup>

*WALL-E* was released in Japan in a limited edition Blu-ray box set, which included a book featuring artwork from the film, a framed print, and a remote control version of Wall-E. *WALL-E* was released by Disney on [4K Blu-ray](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ultra_HD_Blu-ray "Ultra HD Blu-ray") on March 3, 2020.<sup id="cite_ref-93"><a href="https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/WALL-E#cite_note-93">[93]</a></sup>

At the request of Stanton, Disney licensed *WALL-E* to [The Criterion Collection](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/The_Criterion_Collection "The Criterion Collection") in September 2022, who created a special [4K Blu-ray](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ultra_HD_Blu-ray "Ultra HD Blu-ray")\-Blu-ray combo edition of the film that was released on November 22, 2022, featuring the same 4K digital master used for Disney's original 4K Blu-ray release, but now presented in [Dolby Vision](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dolby_Vision "Dolby Vision") and [HDR10+](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/HDR10%2B "HDR10+") as approved by Stanton, along with additional special features. This makes it both the first Pixar feature film and the eighth Walt Disney Pictures film to be released<sup id="cite_ref-Criterion_94-0"><a href="https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/WALL-E#cite_note-Criterion-94">[a]</a></sup> under the Criterion label.<sup id="cite_ref-:1_95-0"><a href="https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/WALL-E#cite_note-:1-95">[94]</a></sup> In its description for the release, Criterion viewed the film as an important work of both the animation medium and of cinema itself, saying that while "Transporting us simultaneously back to cinema’s silent origins and light-years into the future, *WALL-E* is a soaring ode to the power of love and art to heal a dying world."<sup id="cite_ref-96"><a href="https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/WALL-E#cite_note-96">[95]</a></sup><sup id="cite_ref-97"><a href="https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/WALL-E#cite_note-97">[96]</a></sup><sup id="cite_ref-:1_95-1"><a href="https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/WALL-E#cite_note-:1-95">[94]</a></sup>

## Reception

### Box office

*WALL-E* grossed $223.8 million in the United States and Canada and $308.7 million overseas, for a worldwide total of $532.5 million,<sup id="cite_ref-BOM_3-1"><a href="https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/WALL-E#cite_note-BOM-3">[3]</a></sup> making it the ninth-highest-grossing film of 2008.<sup id="cite_ref-98"><a href="https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/WALL-E#cite_note-98">[97]</a></sup>

In the US and Canada, *WALL-E* opened in 3,992 theaters on June 27, 2008.<sup id="cite_ref-BOMOpen_99-0"><a href="https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/WALL-E#cite_note-BOMOpen-99">[98]</a></sup> The film grossed $23.1 million on its opening day, the highest of all nine Pixar titles to date.<sup id="cite_ref-DeadlineOpen_100-0"><a href="https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/WALL-E#cite_note-DeadlineOpen-100">[99]</a></sup> During its opening weekend, it topped the box office with $63,087,526.<sup id="cite_ref-BOMOpen_99-1"><a href="https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/WALL-E#cite_note-BOMOpen-99">[98]</a></sup> This was the third-best opening weekend for a Pixar film after *[Finding Nemo](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Finding_Nemo "Finding Nemo")* and *[The Incredibles](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/The_Incredibles "The Incredibles")*, and the second-best opening weekend among films released in June, behind *[Harry Potter and the Prisoner of Azkaban](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Harry_Potter_and_the_Prisoner_of_Azkaban_(film) "Harry Potter and the Prisoner of Azkaban (film)")*.<sup id="cite_ref-DeadlineOpen_100-1"><a href="https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/WALL-E#cite_note-DeadlineOpen-100">[99]</a></sup> The film grossed $38 million the following weekend, losing its first place to *[Hancock](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hancock_(film) "Hancock (film)")*.<sup id="cite_ref-101"><a href="https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/WALL-E#cite_note-101">[100]</a></sup> *WALL-E* crossed the $200 million mark by August 3, during its sixth weekend.<sup id="cite_ref-102"><a href="https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/WALL-E#cite_note-102">[101]</a></sup>

*WALL-E* grossed over $10 million in Japan ($44,005,222), UK, Ireland and [Malta](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Malta "Malta") ($41,215,600), France and the [Maghreb](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Maghreb "Maghreb") region ($27,984,103), Germany ($24,130,400), [Mexico](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mexico "Mexico") ($17,679,805), Spain ($14,973,097), Australia ($14,165,390), Italy ($12,210,993), and Russia and the [CIS](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Commonwealth_of_Independent_States "Commonwealth of Independent States") ($11,694,482).<sup id="cite_ref-103"><a href="https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/WALL-E#cite_note-103">[102]</a></sup>

### Critical response

The [American Film Institute](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/American_Film_Institute "American Film Institute") named *WALL-E* as one of the best films of 2008;<sup id="cite_ref-104"><a href="https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/WALL-E#cite_note-104">[103]</a></sup> the jury rationale states:

> *WALL•E* proves to this generation and beyond that the film medium's only true boundaries are the human imagination. Writer/director Andrew Stanton and his team have created a classic screen character from a metal trash compactor who rides to the rescue of a planet buried in the debris that embodies the broken promise of American life. Not since Chaplin's "[Little Tramp](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/The_Tramp "The Tramp")" has so much story—so much emotion—been conveyed without words. When hope arrives in the form of a seedling, the film blossoms into one of the great screen romances as two robots remind audiences of the beating heart in all of us that yearns for humanity—and love—in the darkest of landscapes.

On the [review aggregator](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Review_aggregator "Review aggregator") website [Rotten Tomatoes](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Rotten_Tomatoes "Rotten Tomatoes"), 95% of 262 critics' reviews are positive, with an average rating of 8.6/10. The website's consensus reads: "*Wall-E*'s stellar visuals testify once again to Pixar's ingenuity, while its charming star will captivate younger viewers—and its timely story offers thought-provoking subtext."<sup id="cite_ref-rt_105-0"><a href="https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/WALL-E#cite_note-rt-105">[104]</a></sup> At [Metacritic](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Metacritic "Metacritic"), which assigns a normalized rating to reviews from mainstream critics, the film has an [average](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Weighted_mean "Weighted mean") score of 95 out of 100 based on 39 reviews, indicating "universal acclaim".<sup id="cite_ref-meta_106-0"><a href="https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/WALL-E#cite_note-meta-106">[105]</a></sup> Audiences polled by [CinemaScore](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/CinemaScore "CinemaScore") gave the film an average rating of "A" on an A+ to F scale.<sup id="cite_ref-107"><a href="https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/WALL-E#cite_note-107">[106]</a></sup>

*[IndieWire](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/IndieWire "IndieWire")* named *WALL-E* the third-best film of the year based on their annual survey of 100 film critics, while Movie City News shows that *WALL-E* appeared in 162 different top 10 lists, out of 286 different critics lists surveyed, the most mentions on a top 10 list of any film released in 2008.<sup id="cite_ref-108"><a href="https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/WALL-E#cite_note-108">[107]</a></sup>

[Richard Corliss](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Richard_Corliss "Richard Corliss") of *[Time](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Time_(magazine) "Time (magazine)")* named *WALL-E* his favorite film of 2008 (and later of the decade), noting the film succeeded in "connect\[ing\] with a huge audience" despite the main characters' lack of speech and "emotional signifiers like a mouth, eyebrows, shoulders, \[and\] elbows". It "evoke\[d\] the splendor of the movie past" and he also compared WALL-E and EVE's relationship to the chemistry of [Spencer Tracy](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Spencer_Tracy "Spencer Tracy") and [Katharine Hepburn](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Katharine_Hepburn "Katharine Hepburn").<sup id="cite_ref-109"><a href="https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/WALL-E#cite_note-109">[108]</a></sup> Other critics who named *WALL-E* their favorite film of 2008 included Tom Charity of [CNN](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/CNN "CNN"),<sup id="cite_ref-110"><a href="https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/WALL-E#cite_note-110">[109]</a></sup> Michael Phillips of the *[Chicago Tribune](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Chicago_Tribune "Chicago Tribune")*, Lisa Schwarzbaum of *[Entertainment Weekly](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Entertainment_Weekly "Entertainment Weekly")*, [A. O. Scott](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/A._O._Scott "A. O. Scott") of *[The New York Times](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/The_New_York_Times "The New York Times")*, [Christopher Orr](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Christopher_Orr_(film_critic) "Christopher Orr (film critic)") of *[The New Republic](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/The_New_Republic "The New Republic")*, [Ty Burr](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ty_Burr "Ty Burr") and [Wesley Morris](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Wesley_Morris "Wesley Morris") of *[The Boston Globe](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/The_Boston_Globe "The Boston Globe")*, [Joe Morgenstern](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Joe_Morgenstern "Joe Morgenstern") of *[The Wall Street Journal](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/The_Wall_Street_Journal "The Wall Street Journal")*, and [Anthony Lane](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Anthony_Lane "Anthony Lane") of *[The New Yorker](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/The_New_Yorker "The New Yorker")*.<sup id="cite_ref-111"><a href="https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/WALL-E#cite_note-111">[110]</a></sup>

Todd McCarthy of *[Variety](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Variety_(magazine) "Variety (magazine)")* called the film "Pixar's ninth consecutive wonder", saying it was imaginative yet straightforward. He said it pushed the boundaries of animation by balancing esoteric ideas with more immediately accessible ones, and that the main difference between the film and other science fiction projects rooted in an [apocalypse](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Apocalypse "Apocalypse") was its optimism.<sup id="cite_ref-var_112-0"><a href="https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/WALL-E#cite_note-var-112">[111]</a></sup> Kirk Honeycutt of *[The Hollywood Reporter](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/The_Hollywood_Reporter "The Hollywood Reporter")* declared that *WALL-E* surpassed the achievements of Pixar's previous eight features and probably their most original film to date. He said it had the "heart, soul, spirit and romance" of the best [silent films](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Silent_film "Silent film"). Honeycutt said the film's definitive stroke of brilliance was in using a mix of archive film footage and computer graphics to trigger WALL·E's romantic leanings. He praised Burtt's sound design, saying "If there is such a thing as an aural sleight of hand, this is it."<sup id="cite_ref-113"><a href="https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/WALL-E#cite_note-113">[112]</a></sup>

[Roger Ebert](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Roger_Ebert "Roger Ebert") of the *[Chicago Sun-Times](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Chicago_Sun-Times "Chicago Sun-Times")* named *WALL-E* "an enthralling animated film, a visual wonderment, and a decent science-fiction story" and said the scarcity of dialogue would allow it to "cross language barriers" in a manner appropriate to the global theme, and noted it would appeal to adults and children. He praised the animation, describing the color palette as "bright and cheerful ... and a little bit realistic", and that Pixar managed to generate a "curious" regard for the WALL·E, comparing his "rusty and hard-working and plucky" design favorably to more obvious attempts at creating "lovable" lead characters. He said *WALL-E* was concerned with ideas rather than spectacle, saying it would trigger stimulating "little thoughts for the younger viewers."<sup id="cite_ref-114"><a href="https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/WALL-E#cite_note-114">[113]</a></sup> He named it as one of his twenty favorite films of 2008 and argued it was "the best science-fiction movie in years".<sup id="cite_ref-115"><a href="https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/WALL-E#cite_note-115">[114]</a></sup>

The film was interpreted as tackling a topical, [ecologically](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ecologically "Ecologically")\-minded agenda,<sup id="cite_ref-rt_105-1"><a href="https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/WALL-E#cite_note-rt-105">[104]</a></sup> though McCarthy said it did so with a lightness of touch that granted the viewer the ability to accept or ignore the message.<sup id="cite_ref-var_112-1"><a href="https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/WALL-E#cite_note-var-112">[111]</a></sup> [Kyle Smith](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Kyle_Smith_(critic) "Kyle Smith (critic)") of the *[New York Post](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/New_York_Post "New York Post")*, wrote that by depicting future humans as "a flabby mass of peabrained idiots who are literally too fat to walk", *WALL-E* was darker and more cynical than any major Disney feature film he could recall. He compared the humans to the patrons of [Disney's theme parks and resorts](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Disney_Parks,_Experiences_and_Products "Disney Parks, Experiences and Products"), adding, "I'm also not sure I've ever seen a major corporation spend so much money to issue an insult to its customers."<sup id="cite_ref-116"><a href="https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/WALL-E#cite_note-116">[115]</a></sup> Maura Judkis of *[U.S. News & World Report](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/U.S._News_%26_World_Report "U.S. News & World Report")* questioned whether this depiction of "frighteningly obese humans" would resonate with children and make them prefer to "play outside rather than in front of the computer, to avoid a similar fate".<sup id="cite_ref-117"><a href="https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/WALL-E#cite_note-117">[116]</a></sup>

A few notable critics have argued that the film is vastly overrated,<sup id="cite_ref-forward.com_118-0"><a href="https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/WALL-E#cite_note-forward.com-118">[117]</a></sup> claiming it failed to "live up to such blinding, high-wattage enthusiasm",<sup id="cite_ref-ft.com_119-0"><a href="https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/WALL-E#cite_note-ft.com-119">[118]</a></sup> and that there were "chasms of boredom watching it", in particular "the second and third acts spiraled into the expected".<sup id="cite_ref-120"><a href="https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/WALL-E#cite_note-120">[119]</a></sup> Other labels included "preachy"<sup id="cite_ref-forward.com_118-1"><a href="https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/WALL-E#cite_note-forward.com-118">[117]</a></sup> and "too long".<sup id="cite_ref-ft.com_119-1"><a href="https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/WALL-E#cite_note-ft.com-119">[118]</a></sup> Child reviews sent into [CBBC](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/CBBC_(TV_channel) "CBBC (TV channel)") were mixed, some citing boredom and an inadequate storyline.<sup id="cite_ref-121"><a href="https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/WALL-E#cite_note-121">[120]</a></sup>

Several [conservative](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Conservatism_in_the_United_States "Conservatism in the United States") commentators criticized the film. [Shannen W. Coffin](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Shannen_W._Coffin "Shannen W. Coffin") of *[National Review](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/National_Review "National Review")* said that *WALL-E* is "leftist propaganda about the evils of mankind"*.* [Greg Pollowitz](https://en.wikipedia.org/w/index.php?title=Greg_Pollowitz&action=edit&redlink=1 "Greg Pollowitz (page does not exist)") of *National Review* called the film "a 90-minute lecture on the dangers of over consumption, big corporations, and the destruction of the environment". [Jonah Goldberg](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Jonah_Goldberg "Jonah Goldberg") said that while the film was "fascinating and at-times brilliant", he added that it is also "[Malthusian](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Malthusianism "Malthusianism") fear mongering". [Glenn Beck](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Glenn_Beck "Glenn Beck") said that "I can't wait to teach my kids how we've destroyed the Earth ... Pixar is teaching. I can't wait. You know if your kid has ever come home and said, 'Dad, how come we use so much [styrofoam](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Styrofoam "Styrofoam"),' oh, this is the movie for you."<sup id="cite_ref-122"><a href="https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/WALL-E#cite_note-122">[121]</a></sup><sup id="cite_ref-123"><a href="https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/WALL-E#cite_note-123">[122]</a></sup>

Patrick J. Ford of *[The American Conservative](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/The_American_Conservative "The American Conservative")* said *WALL-E*'s conservative critics missed lessons in the film that he felt appealed to [traditional conservatism](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Traditional_conservatism "Traditional conservatism"). He argued that the mass [consumerism](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Consumerism "Consumerism") in the film was not shown to be a product of [big business](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Big_business "Big business"), but of too close a tie between big business and [big government](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Big_government "Big government"): "The government unilaterally provided its citizens with everything they needed, and this lack of variety led to Earth's downfall." Responding to Coffin's claim that the film points out the evils of mankind, Ford argued the only evils depicted were those that resulted from losing touch with our own humanity and that fundamental conservative representations such as the farm, the nuclear family unit, and "wholesome entertainment" were seen as "beautiful and desirable." by the human characters. He concluded, "By steering conservative families away from *WALL-E*, these commentators are doing their readers a great disservice."<sup id="cite_ref-ford_124-0"><a href="https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/WALL-E#cite_note-ford-124">[123]</a></sup>

Director [Terry Gilliam](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Terry_Gilliam "Terry Gilliam") praised the film as "A stunning bit of work. The scenes on what was left of planet Earth are just so beautiful: one of the great silent movies. And the most stunning artwork! It says more about ecology and society than any live-action film—all the people on their loungers floating around, brilliant stuff. Their social comment was so smart and right on the button."<sup id="cite_ref-125"><a href="https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/WALL-E#cite_note-125">[124]</a></sup>

Archaeologists have commented on the themes of human evolution that the film explores.<sup id="cite_ref-marwick_126-0"><a href="https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/WALL-E#cite_note-marwick-126">[125]</a></sup> Ben Marwick has written how the character of WALL-E resembles an archaeologist with his methodical collection and classification of quotidian human artefacts. He is shown facing a typological dilemma of classifying a [spork](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Spork "Spork") as either a fork or spoon, and his nostalgic interest in the human past further demonstrated by his attachment to repeated viewings of the 1969 film [*Hello, Dolly!*](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hello,_Dolly!_(film) "Hello, Dolly! (film)"). Marwick notes that the film features major human evolutionary transitions such as obligate bipedalism (captain of the spaceship struggles with the autopilot to gain control of the vessel) and the invention of agriculture, as part of watershed moments in the story of the film. According to Marwick, one prominent message of the film "appears to be that the envelopment by technology that the humans in *Wall-E* experience paradoxically results in physical and cultural devolution." Scholars such as [Ian Tattersall](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ian_Tattersall "Ian Tattersall") and [Steve Jones](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Steve_Jones_(biologist) "Steve Jones (biologist)") have similarly discussed scenarios where elements of modern technology (such as medicine) may have caused [human evolution](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Human_evolution "Human evolution") to slow or stop.

In 2021, the film was selected for preservation in the United States [National Film Registry](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/National_Film_Registry "National Film Registry") by the [Library of Congress](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Library_of_Congress "Library of Congress") as being "culturally, historically, or aesthetically significant".<sup id="cite_ref-Tartaglione_16-1"><a href="https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/WALL-E#cite_note-Tartaglione-16">[16]</a></sup>

### Accolades

*WALL-E* won the [Academy Award for Best Animated Feature](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Academy_Award_for_Best_Animated_Feature "Academy Award for Best Animated Feature") and was nominated for [Best Original Screenplay](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Academy_Award_for_Best_Original_Screenplay "Academy Award for Best Original Screenplay"), [Best Original Score](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Academy_Award_for_Best_Original_Score "Academy Award for Best Original Score"), [Best Original Song](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Academy_Award_for_Best_Original_Song "Academy Award for Best Original Song"), [Sound Editing](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Academy_Award_for_Sound_Editing "Academy Award for Sound Editing"), and [Sound Mixing](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Academy_Award_for_Best_Sound "Academy Award for Best Sound") at the [81st Academy Awards](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/81st_Academy_Awards "81st Academy Awards").<sup id="cite_ref-Oscars2009_127-0"><a href="https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/WALL-E#cite_note-Oscars2009-127">[126]</a></sup><sup id="cite_ref-128"><a href="https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/WALL-E#cite_note-128">[127]</a></sup> Walt Disney Pictures also pushed for an [Academy Award for Best Picture](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Academy_Award_for_Best_Picture "Academy Award for Best Picture") nomination,<sup id="cite_ref-129"><a href="https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/WALL-E#cite_note-129">[128]</a></sup> but it was not nominated, sparking controversy over whether the Academy deliberately restricted *WALL-E* to the Best Animated Feature category.<sup id="cite_ref-130"><a href="https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/WALL-E#cite_note-130">[129]</a></sup> Film critic [Peter Travers](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Peter_Travers "Peter Travers") remarked, "If there was ever a time where an animated feature deserved to be nominated for best picture it's *Wall-E*."<sup id="cite_ref-131"><a href="https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/WALL-E#cite_note-131">[130]</a></sup> Only three animated films, 1991's *[Beauty and the Beast](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Beauty_and_the_Beast_(1991_film) "Beauty and the Beast (1991 film)")* and Pixar's next two films, 2009's *[Up](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Up_(2009_film) "Up (2009 film)")* and 2010's *[Toy Story 3](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Toy_Story_3 "Toy Story 3")*, have ever been nominated for the Academy Award for Best Picture. A reflective Stanton stated he was not disappointed the film was restricted to the Best Animated Film nomination because he was overwhelmed by the film's positive reception, and eventually "The line \[between live-action and animation\] is just getting so blurry that I think with each proceeding year, it's going to be tougher and tougher to say what's an animated movie and what's not an animated movie."<sup id="cite_ref-ball_31-1"><a href="https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/WALL-E#cite_note-ball-31">[31]</a></sup>

*WALL-E* made a healthy appearance at the various 2008 end-of-the-year awards circles, particularly in the Best Picture category, where animated films are often overlooked. It has won the award, or the equivalent of it, from the [Boston Society of Film Critics](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Boston_Society_of_Film_Critics "Boston Society of Film Critics") (tied with *[Slumdog Millionaire](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Slumdog_Millionaire "Slumdog Millionaire")*),<sup id="cite_ref-132"><a href="https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/WALL-E#cite_note-132">[131]</a></sup> the [Chicago Film Critics Association](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Chicago_Film_Critics_Association "Chicago Film Critics Association"),<sup id="cite_ref-133"><a href="https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/WALL-E#cite_note-133">[132]</a></sup> the Central Ohio Film Critics awards,<sup id="cite_ref-134"><a href="https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/WALL-E#cite_note-134">[133]</a></sup> the [Online Film Critics Society](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Online_Film_Critics_Society "Online Film Critics Society"),<sup id="cite_ref-135"><a href="https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/WALL-E#cite_note-135">[134]</a></sup> and most notably the [Los Angeles Film Critics Association](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Los_Angeles_Film_Critics_Association "Los Angeles Film Critics Association"), where it became the first animated feature to win the prestigious award.<sup id="cite_ref-136"><a href="https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/WALL-E#cite_note-136">[135]</a></sup> It was named as one of 2008's ten best films by the [American Film Institute](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/American_Film_Institute "American Film Institute") and the [National Board of Review of Motion Pictures](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/National_Board_of_Review_of_Motion_Pictures "National Board of Review of Motion Pictures").<sup id="cite_ref-137"><a href="https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/WALL-E#cite_note-137">[136]</a></sup><sup id="cite_ref-138"><a href="https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/WALL-E#cite_note-138">[137]</a></sup>

## Robotic recreations

In 2012, Mike McMaster,<sup id="cite_ref-155"><a href="https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/WALL-E#cite_note-155">[154]</a></sup> an American robotics hobbyist, began working on his own model of [WALL·E](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/WALL-E_(character) "WALL-E (character)"). The final product was built with more moving parts than the WALL·E which roams around [Disneyland](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Disneyland "Disneyland"). McMaster's four-foot robot made an appearance at the [Walt Disney Family Museum](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Walt_Disney_Family_Museum "Walt Disney Family Museum") and was featured during the opening week of Tested.com<sup id="cite_ref-156"><a href="https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/WALL-E#cite_note-156">[155]</a></sup> a project headed up by [Jamie Hyneman](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Jamie_Hyneman "Jamie Hyneman") and [Adam Savage](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Adam_Savage "Adam Savage") of *[MythBusters](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/MythBusters "MythBusters")*. Since WALL-E's creation, Mike and the popular robot have made dozens of appearances at various events.<sup id="cite_ref-157"><a href="https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/WALL-E#cite_note-157">[156]</a></sup>

In the same year, Mike Senna completed his own WALL·E build.<sup id="cite_ref-158"><a href="https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/WALL-E#cite_note-158">[157]</a></sup> He also created an EVE. They were present at a photo op at Disney's D23 Expo 2015.<sup id="cite_ref-159"><a href="https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/WALL-E#cite_note-159">[158]</a></sup>
