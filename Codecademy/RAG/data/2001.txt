***2001: A Space Odyssey*** is a 1968 [epic](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Epic_film "Epic film") [science fiction film](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Science_fiction_film "Science fiction film") produced and directed by [Stanley Kubrick](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Stanley_Kubrick "Stanley Kubrick"). The [screenplay](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Screenplay "Screenplay") was written by Kubrick and science fiction author [Arthur C. Clarke](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Arthur_C._Clarke "Arthur C. Clarke"), and was inspired by Clarke's 1951 short story "[The Sentinel](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/The_Sentinel_(short_story) "The Sentinel (short story)")" and other short stories by Clarke. Clarke also published a [novelisation of the film](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/2001:_A_Space_Odyssey_(novel) "2001: A Space Odyssey (novel)"), in part written concurrently with the screenplay, after the film's release. The film stars [Keir Dullea](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Keir_Dullea "Keir Dullea"), [Gary Lockwood](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Gary_Lockwood "Gary Lockwood"), [William Sylvester](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/William_Sylvester "William Sylvester"), and [Douglas Rain](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Douglas_Rain "Douglas Rain") and follows a voyage by astronauts, scientists, and the [sentient](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sentience "Sentience") supercomputer [HAL](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/HAL_9000 "HAL 9000") to [Jupiter](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Jupiter "Jupiter") to investigate an [alien monolith](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Monolith_(Space_Odyssey) "Monolith (Space Odyssey)").

The film is noted for its scientifically accurate depiction of space flight, pioneering [special effects](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Special_effects "Special effects"), and ambiguous imagery. Kubrick avoided conventional cinematic and narrative techniques; dialogue is used sparingly, and there are long sequences accompanied only by music. The soundtrack incorporates numerous works of [classical music](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Classical_music "Classical music"), including pieces by composers such as [Richard Strauss](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Richard_Strauss "Richard Strauss"), [Johann Strauss II](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Johann_Strauss_II "Johann Strauss II"), [Aram Khachaturian](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Aram_Khachaturian "Aram Khachaturian"), and [György Ligeti](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Gy%C3%B6rgy_Ligeti "György Ligeti").

The film received diverse critical responses, ranging from those who saw it as darkly apocalyptic to those who saw it as an optimistic reappraisal of the hopes of humanity. Critics noted its exploration of themes such as [human evolution](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Human_evolution "Human evolution"), [technology](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Technology "Technology"), [artificial intelligence](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Artificial_intelligence "Artificial intelligence"), and the possibility of [extraterrestrial life](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Extraterrestrial_life "Extraterrestrial life"). It was nominated for four [Academy Awards](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Academy_Awards "Academy Awards"), winning Kubrick the award for his direction of the [visual effects](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Academy_Award_for_Best_Visual_Effects "Academy Award for Best Visual Effects").<sup id="cite_ref-3"><a href="https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/2001:_A_Space_Odyssey#cite_note-3">[3]</a></sup> The film is now widely regarded as one of the [greatest and most influential films ever made](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/List_of_films_considered_the_best "List of films considered the best"). In 1991, it was selected by the United States [Library of Congress](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Library_of_Congress "Library of Congress") for preservation in the [National Film Registry](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/National_Film_Registry "National Film Registry"). In 2022, *2001: A Space Odyssey* placed in the top ten of *[Sight & Sound](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sight_%26_Sound "Sight & Sound")*'s decennial critics' poll, and topped their directors' poll. A sequel, *[2010: The Year We Make Contact](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/2010:_The_Year_We_Make_Contact "2010: The Year We Make Contact")*, was released in 1984, based on the novel *[2010: Odyssey Two](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/2010:_Odyssey_Two "2010: Odyssey Two")*.

## Plot\[[edit](https://en.wikipedia.org/w/index.php?title=2001:_A_Space_Odyssey&action=edit&section=1 "Edit section: Plot")\]

In a prehistoric [veld](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Veld "Veld"), a tribe of [hominins](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hominini "Hominini") is driven away from its water hole by a rival tribe. The next day, they find an [alien monolith](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Monolith_(Space_Odyssey) "Monolith (Space Odyssey)") has appeared in their midst. The tribe then learn how to use a bone as a weapon and, after their first hunt, return to drive their rivals away with it.

Millions of years later, Dr Heywood Floyd, Chairman of the United States National Council of Astronautics, travels to [Clavius](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Clavius_(crater) "Clavius (crater)") Base, an American [lunar outpost](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lunar_outpost "Lunar outpost"). During a stopover at Space Station Five, he meets Russian scientists who are concerned that Clavius seems to be unresponsive. He refuses to discuss rumours of an epidemic at the base. At Clavius, Heywood addresses a meeting of personnel to whom he stresses the need for secrecy regarding their newest discovery. His mission is to investigate a recently found artefact, a monolith buried four million years earlier near the lunar crater [Tycho](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tycho_(lunar_crater) "Tycho (lunar crater)"). As he and others examine the object and are taking photographs, it emits a high-powered radio signal.

Eighteen months later, the American spacecraft *[Discovery One](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Discovery_One "Discovery One")* is bound for [Jupiter](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Jupiter "Jupiter"), with mission pilots and scientists Dr Dave Bowman and Dr Frank Poole on board, along with three other scientists in [suspended animation](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Suspended_animation "Suspended animation"). Most of *Discovery*'s operations are controlled by HAL, a [HAL 9000](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/HAL_9000 "HAL 9000") computer with a human-like personality. When HAL reports the imminent failure of an antenna control device, Dave retrieves it in an [extravehicular activity](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Extravehicular_activity "Extravehicular activity") (EVA) pod, but finds nothing wrong. HAL suggests reinstalling the device and letting it fail so the problem can be verified. [Mission Control](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mission_Control "Mission Control") advises the astronauts that results from their backup 9000 computer indicate that HAL has made an error, but HAL blames it on human error. Concerned about HAL's behaviour, Dave and Frank enter an EVA pod so they can talk in private without HAL overhearing. They agree to disconnect HAL if he is proven wrong. HAL follows their conversation by [lip reading](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lip_reading "Lip reading").

While Frank is floating away from his pod to replace the antenna unit, HAL takes control of the pod and attacks him, sending Frank tumbling away from the ship with a severed air line. Dave takes another pod to rescue Frank. While he is outside, HAL turns off the [life support](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Life_support "Life support") functions of the crewmen in suspended animation, killing them. When Dave returns to the ship with Frank's body, HAL refuses to let him back in, stating that their plan to deactivate him jeopardises the mission. Dave releases Frank's body and opens the ship's emergency airlock with his [remote manipulators](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Remote_manipulator "Remote manipulator"). Lacking a helmet for his spacesuit, he positions his pod carefully so that when he jettisons the pod's door, he is propelled by the escaping air across the vacuum into *Discovery*'s airlock. He enters HAL's [processor core](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Processor_core "Processor core") and begins disconnecting most of HAL's circuits, ignoring HAL's pleas to stop. When he is finished, a prerecorded video by Heywood plays, revealing that the mission's actual objective is to investigate the radio signal sent from the monolith to Jupiter.

At Jupiter, Dave finds a third, much larger monolith orbiting the planet. He leaves *Discovery* in an EVA pod to investigate. He is pulled into a vortex of coloured light and observes bizarre astronomical phenomena and strange landscapes of unusual colours as he passes by. Finally he finds himself in a large [neoclassical](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Neoclassical_architecture "Neoclassical architecture") bedroom where he sees, and then becomes, older versions of himself: first standing in the bedroom, middle-aged and still in his spacesuit, then dressed in leisure attire and eating dinner, and finally as an old man lying in bed. A monolith appears at the foot of the bed, and as Dave reaches for it, he is transformed into a [foetus](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Foetus "Foetus") enclosed in a transparent orb of light floating in space above the Earth.

## Production\[[edit](https://en.wikipedia.org/w/index.php?title=2001:_A_Space_Odyssey&action=edit&section=3 "Edit section: Production")\]

### Development\[[edit](https://en.wikipedia.org/w/index.php?title=2001:_A_Space_Odyssey&action=edit&section=4 "Edit section: Development")\]

After completing *[Dr. Strangelove](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dr._Strangelove "Dr. Strangelove")* (1964), director [Stanley Kubrick](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Stanley_Kubrick "Stanley Kubrick") told a publicist from [Columbia Pictures](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Columbia_Pictures "Columbia Pictures") that his next project would be about [extraterrestrial life](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Extraterrestrial_life "Extraterrestrial life"),<sup id="cite_ref-9"><a href="https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/2001:_A_Space_Odyssey#cite_note-9">[9]</a></sup><sup id="cite_ref-10"><a href="https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/2001:_A_Space_Odyssey#cite_note-10">[10]</a></sup> and resolved to make "the proverbial good science fiction movie".<sup id="cite_ref-Lost_Worlds_11-0"><a href="https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/2001:_A_Space_Odyssey#cite_note-Lost_Worlds-11">[11]</a></sup> How Kubrick became interested in creating a science fiction film is far from clear.<sup id="cite_ref-Baxter_12-0"><a href="https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/2001:_A_Space_Odyssey#cite_note-Baxter-12">[12]</a></sup> Biographer [John Baxter](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/John_Baxter_(author) "John Baxter (author)") notes possible inspirations in the late 1950s, including British productions featuring dramas on satellites and aliens modifying early humans, [Metro-Goldwyn-Mayer](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Metro-Goldwyn-Mayer "Metro-Goldwyn-Mayer")'s big budget [CinemaScope](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/CinemaScope "CinemaScope") production *[Forbidden Planet](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Forbidden_Planet "Forbidden Planet")*, and the slick widescreen cinematography and set design of Japanese *[kaiju](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Kaiju "Kaiju")* (monster movie) productions (such as [Ishirō Honda](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ishir%C5%8D_Honda "Ishirō Honda") and [Eiji Tsuburaya](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Eiji_Tsuburaya "Eiji Tsuburaya")'s *[Godzilla](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Godzilla_(franchise) "Godzilla (franchise)")* films and [Koji Shima](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Koji_Shima "Koji Shima")'s *[Warning from Space](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Warning_from_Space "Warning from Space")*).<sup id="cite_ref-Baxter_12-1"><a href="https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/2001:_A_Space_Odyssey#cite_note-Baxter-12">[12]</a></sup>

Kubrick obtained financing and distribution from the American studio Metro-Goldwyn-Mayer with the selling point that the film could be marketed in their ultra-widescreen [Cinerama](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cinerama "Cinerama") format, recently debuted with their *[How the West Was Won](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/How_the_West_Was_Won_(film) "How the West Was Won (film)")*.<sup id="cite_ref-ChapmanCull2013-p97_13-0"><a href="https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/2001:_A_Space_Odyssey#cite_note-ChapmanCull2013-p97-13">[13]</a></sup><sup id="cite_ref-14"><a href="https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/2001:_A_Space_Odyssey#cite_note-14">[14]</a></sup><sup id="cite_ref-Baxter_12-2"><a href="https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/2001:_A_Space_Odyssey#cite_note-Baxter-12">[12]</a></sup> It would be filmed and edited almost entirely in southern England, where Kubrick lived, using the facilities of [MGM-British Studios](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/MGM-British_Studios "MGM-British Studios") and [Shepperton Studios](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Shepperton_Studios "Shepperton Studios"). MGM had subcontracted the production of the film to Kubrick's production company to qualify for the [Eady Levy](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Eady_Levy "Eady Levy"), a UK tax on box-office receipts used at the time to fund the production of films in Britain.<sup id="cite_ref-ChapmanCull2013-p98_15-0"><a href="https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/2001:_A_Space_Odyssey#cite_note-ChapmanCull2013-p98-15">[15]</a></sup> In a draft version of a contract with Kubrick's production company in May 1965, MGM suggested [Alfred Hitchcock](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Alfred_Hitchcock "Alfred Hitchcock"), [Billy Wilder](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Billy_Wilder "Billy Wilder") and [David Lean](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/David_Lean "David Lean") as possible replacements for Kubrick if he was unavailable.<sup id="cite_ref-16"><a href="https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/2001:_A_Space_Odyssey#cite_note-16">[16]</a></sup>

### Pre-production\[[edit](https://en.wikipedia.org/w/index.php?title=2001:_A_Space_Odyssey&action=edit&section=5 "Edit section: Pre-production")\]

Kubrick's decision to avoid the fanciful portrayals of space found in standard popular science fiction films of the time led him to seek more realistic and accurate depictions of space travel. Illustrators such as [Chesley Bonestell](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Chesley_Bonestell "Chesley Bonestell"), Roy Carnon, and Richard McKenna were hired to produce concept drawings, sketches, and paintings of the space technology seen in the film.<sup id="cite_ref-scienceandfilm.org_17-0"><a href="https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/2001:_A_Space_Odyssey#cite_note-scienceandfilm.org-17">[17]</a></sup><sup id="cite_ref-18"><a href="https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/2001:_A_Space_Odyssey#cite_note-18">[18]</a></sup> Two educational films, the [National Film Board of Canada](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/National_Film_Board_of_Canada "National Film Board of Canada")'s 1960 animated short documentary *[Universe](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Universe_(1960_film) "Universe (1960 film)")* and the [1964 New York World's Fair](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/1964_New_York_World%27s_Fair "1964 New York World's Fair") movie *[To the Moon and Beyond](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/To_the_Moon_and_Beyond "To the Moon and Beyond")*, were major influences.<sup id="cite_ref-scienceandfilm.org_17-1"><a href="https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/2001:_A_Space_Odyssey#cite_note-scienceandfilm.org-17">[17]</a></sup>

According to biographer Vincent LoBrutto, *Universe* was a visual inspiration to Kubrick.<sup id="cite_ref-Stanley_Kubrick_19-0"><a href="https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/2001:_A_Space_Odyssey#cite_note-Stanley_Kubrick-19">[19]</a></sup> The 29-minute film, which had also proved popular at [NASA](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/NASA "NASA") for its realistic portrayal of outer space, met "the standard of dynamic visionary realism that he was looking for". Wally Gentleman, one of the special-effects artists on *Universe*, worked briefly on *2001*. Kubrick also asked *Universe* co-director [Colin Low](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Colin_Low_(filmmaker) "Colin Low (filmmaker)") about animation camerawork, with Low recommending British mathematician Brian Salt, with whom Low and [Roman Kroitor](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Roman_Kroitor "Roman Kroitor") had previously worked on the 1957 still-animation documentary *[City of Gold](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/City_of_Gold_(1957_film) "City of Gold (1957 film)")*.<sup id="cite_ref-Graham_20-0"><a href="https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/2001:_A_Space_Odyssey#cite_note-Graham-20">[20]</a></sup><sup id="cite_ref-Evans_21-0"><a href="https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/2001:_A_Space_Odyssey#cite_note-Evans-21">[21]</a></sup> *Universe*'s narrator, actor [Douglas Rain](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Douglas_Rain "Douglas Rain"), was cast as the voice of HAL.<sup id="cite_ref-Lacey_22-0"><a href="https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/2001:_A_Space_Odyssey#cite_note-Lacey-22">[22]</a></sup> For the role of Heywood Floyd, MGM suggested casting a well-known actor such as [Henry Fonda](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Henry_Fonda "Henry Fonda") or [George C. Scott](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/George_C._Scott "George C. Scott").<sup id="cite_ref-23"><a href="https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/2001:_A_Space_Odyssey#cite_note-23">[23]</a></sup>

After pre-production had begun, Kubrick saw *To the Moon and Beyond*, a film shown in the Transportation and Travel building at the [1964 World's Fair](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/1964_World%27s_Fair "1964 World's Fair"). It was filmed in Cinerama 360 and shown in the "Moon Dome". Kubrick hired the company that produced it, Graphic Films Corporation—which had been making films for NASA, the [US Air Force](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/US_Air_force "US Air force"), and various aerospace clients—as a design consultant.<sup id="cite_ref-scienceandfilm.org_17-2"><a href="https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/2001:_A_Space_Odyssey#cite_note-scienceandfilm.org-17">[17]</a></sup> Graphic Films' Con Pederson, [Lester Novros](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lester_Novros "Lester Novros"), and background artist [Douglas Trumbull](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Douglas_Trumbull "Douglas Trumbull") airmailed research-based concept sketches and notes covering the mechanics and physics of space travel, and created [storyboards](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Storyboard "Storyboard") for the space flight sequences in *2001*.<sup id="cite_ref-scienceandfilm.org_17-3"><a href="https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/2001:_A_Space_Odyssey#cite_note-scienceandfilm.org-17">[17]</a></sup> Trumbull became a special effects supervisor on *2001*.<sup id="cite_ref-scienceandfilm.org_17-4"><a href="https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/2001:_A_Space_Odyssey#cite_note-scienceandfilm.org-17">[17]</a></sup>

### Writing\[[edit](https://en.wikipedia.org/w/index.php?title=2001:_A_Space_Odyssey&action=edit&section=6 "Edit section: Writing")\]

Searching for a collaborator in the [science fiction](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Science_fiction "Science fiction") community for the writing of the script, Kubrick was advised by a mutual acquaintance, Columbia Pictures staff member [Roger Caras](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Roger_A._Caras "Roger A. Caras"), to talk to writer [Arthur C. Clarke](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Arthur_C._Clarke "Arthur C. Clarke"), who lived in [Ceylon](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ceylon "Ceylon"). Although convinced that Clarke was "a recluse, a nut who lives in a tree", Kubrick allowed Caras to cable the film proposal to Clarke. Clarke's cabled response stated that he was "frightfully interested in working with \[that\] *enfant terrible*", and added "what makes Kubrick think I'm a recluse?"<sup id="cite_ref-Stanley_Kubrick_19-1"><a href="https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/2001:_A_Space_Odyssey#cite_note-Stanley_Kubrick-19">[19]</a></sup><sup id="cite_ref-24"><a href="https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/2001:_A_Space_Odyssey#cite_note-24">[24]</a></sup> Meeting for the first time at [Trader Vic's](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Trader_Vic%27s "Trader Vic's") in New York on 22 April 1964, the two began discussing the project that would take up the next four years of their lives.<sup id="cite_ref-Clarke_25-0"><a href="https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/2001:_A_Space_Odyssey#cite_note-Clarke-25">[25]</a></sup> Clarke kept a diary throughout his involvement with *2001*, excerpts of which were published in 1972 as *[The Lost Worlds of 2001](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/The_Lost_Worlds_of_2001 "The Lost Worlds of 2001")*.<sup id="cite_ref-26"><a href="https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/2001:_A_Space_Odyssey#cite_note-26">[26]</a></sup>

[![](https://upload.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/thumb/6/62/Arthur_C._Clarke_1965.jpg/220px-Arthur_C._Clarke_1965.jpg)](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/File:Arthur_C._Clarke_1965.jpg)

Arthur C. Clarke in 1965, photographed in the *Discovery*'s pod bay

Kubrick told Clarke he wanted to make a film about "Man's relationship to the universe",<sup id="cite_ref-27"><a href="https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/2001:_A_Space_Odyssey#cite_note-27">[27]</a></sup> and was, in Clarke's words, "determined to create a work of art which would arouse the emotions of wonder, awe ... even, if appropriate, terror".<sup id="cite_ref-Clarke_25-1"><a href="https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/2001:_A_Space_Odyssey#cite_note-Clarke-25">[25]</a></sup> Clarke offered Kubrick six of his short stories, and by May 1964, Kubrick had chosen "[The Sentinel](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/The_Sentinel_(short_story) "The Sentinel (short story)")" as the source material for the film. In search of more material to expand the film's plot, the two spent the rest of 1964 reading books on science and anthropology, screening science fiction films, and brainstorming ideas.<sup id="cite_ref-28"><a href="https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/2001:_A_Space_Odyssey#cite_note-28">[28]</a></sup> They created the plot for *2001* by integrating several different short story plots written by Clarke, along with new plot segments requested by Kubrick for the film development, and then combined them all into a single script for *2001*.<sup id="cite_ref-29"><a href="https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/2001:_A_Space_Odyssey#cite_note-29">[29]</a></sup><sup id="cite_ref-Benson's_Book_30-0"><a href="https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/2001:_A_Space_Odyssey#cite_note-Benson's_Book-30">[30]</a></sup> Clarke said that his 1953 story "[Encounter in the Dawn](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Encounter_in_the_Dawn "Encounter in the Dawn")" inspired the film's "Dawn of Man" sequence.<sup id="cite_ref-31"><a href="https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/2001:_A_Space_Odyssey#cite_note-31">[31]</a></sup>

Kubrick and Clarke privately referred to the project as *How the Solar System Was Won*, a reference to how it was a follow-on to MGM's Cinerama epic *How the West Was Won*.<sup id="cite_ref-Baxter_12-3"><a href="https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/2001:_A_Space_Odyssey#cite_note-Baxter-12">[12]</a></sup> On 23 February 1965, Kubrick issued a press release announcing the title as *Journey Beyond The Stars*.<sup id="cite_ref-32"><a href="https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/2001:_A_Space_Odyssey#cite_note-32">[32]</a></sup> Other titles considered included *Universe*, *Tunnel to the Stars*, and *Planetfall*. Expressing his high expectations for the thematic importance which he associated with the film, in April 1965, eleven months after they began working on the project, Kubrick selected *2001: A Space Odyssey*; Clarke said the title was "entirely" Kubrick's idea.<sup id="cite_ref-33"><a href="https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/2001:_A_Space_Odyssey#cite_note-33">[33]</a></sup> Intending to set the film apart from the "monsters-and-sex" type of science-fiction films of the time, Kubrick used [Homer](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Homer "Homer")'s *[The Odyssey](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/The_Odyssey "The Odyssey")* as both a model of literary merit and a source of inspiration for the title. Kubrick said, "It occurred to us that for the Greeks the vast stretches of the sea must have had the same sort of mystery and remoteness that space has for our generation."<sup id="cite_ref-34"><a href="https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/2001:_A_Space_Odyssey#cite_note-34">[34]</a></sup>

> How much would we appreciate *[La Gioconda](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mona_Lisa "Mona Lisa")* today if [Leonardo](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Leonardo_da_Vinci "Leonardo da Vinci") had written at the bottom of the canvas: "This lady is smiling slightly because she has rotten teeth" — or "because she's hiding a secret from her lover"? It would shut off the viewer's appreciation and shackle him to a reality other than his own. I don't want that to happen to *2001*.

—Stanley Kubrick, *[Playboy](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Playboy "Playboy")*, 1968<sup id="cite_ref-auto_35-0"><a href="https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/2001:_A_Space_Odyssey#cite_note-auto-35">[35]</a></sup>

Originally, Kubrick and Clarke had planned to develop a [*2001* novel](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/2001:_A_Space_Odyssey_(novel) "2001: A Space Odyssey (novel)") first, free of the constraints of film, and then write the screenplay. They planned the writing credits to be "Screenplay by Stanley Kubrick and Arthur C. Clarke, based on a novel by Arthur C. Clarke and Stanley Kubrick" to reflect their preeminence in their respective fields.<sup id="cite_ref-36"><a href="https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/2001:_A_Space_Odyssey#cite_note-36">[36]</a></sup> In practice, the screenplay developed in parallel with the novel, with only some elements being common to both. In a 1970 interview, Kubrick said:

> There are a number of differences between the book and the movie. The novel, for example, attempts to explain things much more explicitly than the film does, which is inevitable in a verbal medium. The novel came about after we did a 130-page prose treatment of the film at the very outset. ... Arthur took all the existing material, plus an impression of some of the rushes, and wrote the novel. As a result, there's a difference between the novel and the film ... I think that the divergences between the two works are interesting.<sup id="cite_ref-Gelmis_37-0"><a href="https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/2001:_A_Space_Odyssey#cite_note-Gelmis-37">[37]</a></sup>

In the end, Clarke and Kubrick wrote parts of the novel and screenplay simultaneously, with the film version being released before the book version was published. Clarke opted for clearer explanations of the mysterious monolith and Star Gate in the novel; Kubrick made the film more cryptic by minimising dialogue and explanation.<sup id="cite_ref-krusch_38-0"><a href="https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/2001:_A_Space_Odyssey#cite_note-krusch-38">[38]</a></sup> Kubrick said the film is "basically a visual, nonverbal experience" that "hits the viewer at an inner level of consciousness, just as music does, or painting".<sup id="cite_ref-39"><a href="https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/2001:_A_Space_Odyssey#cite_note-39">[39]</a></sup>

The screenplay credits were shared whereas the *2001* novel, released shortly after the film, was attributed to Clarke alone. Clarke wrote later that "the nearest approximation to the complicated truth" is that the screenplay should be credited to "Kubrick and Clarke" and the novel to "Clarke and Kubrick".<sup id="cite_ref-Clarke_pp.31-38_40-0"><a href="https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/2001:_A_Space_Odyssey#cite_note-Clarke_pp.31-38-40">[40]</a></sup> Early reports about tensions involved in the writing of the film script appeared to reach a point where Kubrick was allegedly so dissatisfied with the collaboration that he approached other writers who could replace Clarke, including [Michael Moorcock](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Michael_Moorcock "Michael Moorcock") and [J. G. Ballard](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/J._G._Ballard "J. G. Ballard"). But they felt it would be disloyal to accept Kubrick's offer.<sup id="cite_ref-41"><a href="https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/2001:_A_Space_Odyssey#cite_note-41">[41]</a></sup> In [Michael Benson](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Michael_Benson_(filmmaker) "Michael Benson (filmmaker)")'s 2018 book *Space Odyssey: Stanley Kubrick, Arthur C. Clarke, and the Making of a Masterpiece*, the actual relation between Clarke and Kubrick was more complex, involving an extended interaction of Kubrick's multiple requests for Clarke to write new plot lines for various segments of the film, which Clarke was expected to withhold from publication until after the release of the film while receiving advances on his salary from Kubrick during film production. Clarke agreed to this, though apparently he did make several requests for Kubrick to allow him to develop his new plot lines into separate publishable stories while film production continued, which Kubrick consistently denied on the basis of Clarke's contractual obligation to withhold publication until release of the film.<sup id="cite_ref-Benson's_Book_30-1"><a href="https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/2001:_A_Space_Odyssey#cite_note-Benson's_Book-30">[30]</a></sup>

Astronomer [Carl Sagan](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Carl_Sagan "Carl Sagan") wrote in his 1973 book *[The Cosmic Connection](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/The_Cosmic_Connection "The Cosmic Connection")* that Clarke and Kubrick had asked him how to best depict [extraterrestrial intelligence](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Extraterrestrial_intelligence "Extraterrestrial intelligence"). While acknowledging Kubrick's desire to use actors to portray humanoid aliens for convenience's sake, Sagan argued that alien life forms were unlikely to bear any resemblance to terrestrial life, and that to do so would introduce "at least an element of falseness" to the film. Sagan proposed that the film should simply suggest extraterrestrial [superintelligence](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Superintelligence "Superintelligence"), rather than depict it. He attended the premiere and was "pleased to see that I had been of some help".<sup id="cite_ref-42"><a href="https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/2001:_A_Space_Odyssey#cite_note-42">[42]</a></sup> Sagan had met with Clarke and Kubrick only once, in 1964; and Kubrick subsequently directed several attempts to portray credible aliens, only to abandon the idea near the end of post-production. Benson asserts it is unlikely that Sagan's advice had any direct influence.<sup id="cite_ref-Benson's_Book_30-2"><a href="https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/2001:_A_Space_Odyssey#cite_note-Benson's_Book-30">[30]</a></sup> Kubrick hinted at the nature of the mysterious unseen alien race in *2001* by suggesting that given millions of years of evolution, they progressed from biological beings to "immortal machine entities" and then into "beings of pure energy and spirit" with "limitless capabilities and ungraspable intelligence".<sup id="cite_ref-Playboy_43-0"><a href="https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/2001:_A_Space_Odyssey#cite_note-Playboy-43">[43]</a></sup>

In a 1980 interview (not released during Kubrick's lifetime), Kubrick explains one of the film's closing scenes, where Bowman is depicted in old age after his journey through the Star Gate:

> The idea was supposed to be that he is taken in by godlike entities, creatures of pure energy and intelligence with no shape or form. They put him in what I suppose you could describe as a human zoo to study him, and his whole life passes from that point on in that room. And he has no sense of time. ... \[W\]hen they get finished with him, as happens in so many myths of all cultures in the world, he is transformed into some kind of super being and sent back to Earth, transformed and made some kind of superman. We have to only guess what happens when he goes back. It is the pattern of a great deal of mythology, and that is what we were trying to suggest.<sup id="cite_ref-SF-20180706_44-0"><a href="https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/2001:_A_Space_Odyssey#cite_note-SF-20180706-44">[44]</a></sup>

The script went through many stages. In early 1965, when backing was secured for the film, Clarke and Kubrick still had no firm idea of what would happen to Bowman after the Star Gate sequence. Initially all of *Discovery*'s astronauts were to survive the journey; by 3 October, Clarke and Kubrick had decided to make Bowman the sole survivor and have him regress to infancy. By 17 October, Kubrick had come up with what Clarke called a "wild idea of slightly [fag](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Fagging "Fagging") robots who create a [Victorian](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Victorian_era "Victorian era") environment to put our heroes at their ease".<sup id="cite_ref-Clarke_pp.31-38_40-1"><a href="https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/2001:_A_Space_Odyssey#cite_note-Clarke_pp.31-38-40">[40]</a></sup> HAL 9000 was originally named Athena after the [Greek goddess of wisdom](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Athena "Athena") and had a feminine voice and persona.<sup id="cite_ref-Clarke_pp.31-38_40-2"><a href="https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/2001:_A_Space_Odyssey#cite_note-Clarke_pp.31-38-40">[40]</a></sup>

Early drafts included a prologue containing interviews with scientists about extraterrestrial life,<sup id="cite_ref-Agel_p.48_45-0"><a href="https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/2001:_A_Space_Odyssey#cite_note-Agel_p.48-45">[45]</a></sup> [voice-over](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Voice-over "Voice-over") narration (a feature in all of Kubrick's previous films),<sup id="cite_ref-47"><a href="https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/2001:_A_Space_Odyssey#cite_note-47">[a]</a></sup> a stronger emphasis on the prevailing Cold War [balance of terror](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Balance_of_terror "Balance of terror"), and a different and more explicitly explained breakdown for HAL.<sup id="cite_ref-visual-memory_a_48-0"><a href="https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/2001:_A_Space_Odyssey#cite_note-visual-memory_a-48">[47]</a></sup><sup id="cite_ref-Clarke_Novel_49-0"><a href="https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/2001:_A_Space_Odyssey#cite_note-Clarke_Novel-49">[48]</a></sup> Other changes include a different monolith for the "Dawn of Man" sequence, discarded when early prototypes did not photograph well; the use of Saturn as the final destination of the *Discovery* mission rather than Jupiter, discarded when the special effects team could not develop a convincing rendition of [Saturn's rings](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Rings_of_Saturn "Rings of Saturn"); and the finale of the Star Child exploding nuclear weapons carried by Earth-orbiting satellites,<sup id="cite_ref-Clarke_Novel_49-1"><a href="https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/2001:_A_Space_Odyssey#cite_note-Clarke_Novel-49">[48]</a></sup> which Kubrick discarded for its similarity to his previous film, *Dr. Strangelove*.<sup id="cite_ref-Agel_p.48_45-1"><a href="https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/2001:_A_Space_Odyssey#cite_note-Agel_p.48-45">[45]</a></sup><sup id="cite_ref-Clarke_Novel_49-2"><a href="https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/2001:_A_Space_Odyssey#cite_note-Clarke_Novel-49">[48]</a></sup> The finale and many of the other discarded screenplay ideas survived in Clarke's novel.<sup id="cite_ref-Clarke_Novel_49-3"><a href="https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/2001:_A_Space_Odyssey#cite_note-Clarke_Novel-49">[48]</a></sup>

Kubrick made further changes to make the film more nonverbal, to communicate on a visual and visceral level rather than through conventional narrative.<sup id="cite_ref-auto_35-1"><a href="https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/2001:_A_Space_Odyssey#cite_note-auto-35">[35]</a></sup> By the time shooting began, Kubrick had removed much of the dialogue and narration.<sup id="cite_ref-Walker_1971_251_50-0"><a href="https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/2001:_A_Space_Odyssey#cite_note-Walker_1971_251-50">[49]</a></sup> Long periods without dialogue permeate the film: the film has no dialogue for roughly the first and last twenty minutes,<sup id="cite_ref-Ebert_51-0"><a href="https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/2001:_A_Space_Odyssey#cite_note-Ebert-51">[50]</a></sup> as well as for the 10 minutes from Floyd's [Moonbus](https://en.wikipedia.org/w/index.php?title=List_of_spacecraft_from_the_Space_Odyssey_series&action=edit&redlink=1 "List of spacecraft from the Space Odyssey series (page does not exist)") landing near the monolith until Poole watches a BBC newscast on *Discovery*. What dialogue remains is notable for its banality (making the computer HAL seem to have more emotion than the humans) when juxtaposed with the epic space scenes.<sup id="cite_ref-Walker_1971_251_50-1"><a href="https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/2001:_A_Space_Odyssey#cite_note-Walker_1971_251-50">[49]</a></sup> Vincent LoBrutto wrote that Clarke's novel has its own "strong narrative structure" and precision, while the narrative of the film remains symbolic, in accord with Kubrick's final intentions.<sup id="cite_ref-52"><a href="https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/2001:_A_Space_Odyssey#cite_note-52">[51]</a></sup>

### Filming\[[edit](https://en.wikipedia.org/w/index.php?title=2001:_A_Space_Odyssey&action=edit&section=7 "Edit section: Filming")\]

[Principal photography](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Principal_photography "Principal photography") began on 29 December 1965, in Stage H at Shepperton Studios, [Shepperton](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Shepperton "Shepperton"), England. The studio was chosen because it could house the 60-by-120-by-60-foot (18 m × 37 m × 18 m) pit for the [Tycho](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tycho_(crater) "Tycho (crater)") crater excavation scene, the first to be shot. In January 1966, the production moved to the smaller MGM-British Studios in [Borehamwood](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Borehamwood "Borehamwood"), where the live-action and special-effects filming was done, starting with the scenes involving Floyd on the Orion spaceplane;<sup id="cite_ref-53"><a href="https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/2001:_A_Space_Odyssey#cite_note-53">[52]</a></sup> it was described as a "huge throbbing nerve center ... with much the same frenetic atmosphere as a Cape Kennedy blockhouse during the final stages of Countdown."<sup id="cite_ref-filming_2001_54-0"><a href="https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/2001:_A_Space_Odyssey#cite_note-filming_2001-54">[53]</a></sup> The only scene not filmed in a studio—and the last live-action scene shot for the film—was the skull-smashing sequence, in which Moonwatcher (Richter) wields his newfound bone "weapon-tool" against a pile of nearby animal bones. A small elevated platform was built in a field near the studio so that the camera could shoot upward with the sky as background, avoiding cars and trucks passing by in the distance.<sup id="cite_ref-Richter_2002_55-0"><a href="https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/2001:_A_Space_Odyssey#cite_note-Richter_2002-55">[54]</a></sup><sup id="cite_ref-56"><a href="https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/2001:_A_Space_Odyssey#cite_note-56">[55]</a></sup> The *Dawn of Man* sequence that opens the film was shot at Borehamwood with [John Alcott](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/John_Alcott "John Alcott") as cinematographer after [Geoffrey Unsworth](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Geoffrey_Unsworth "Geoffrey Unsworth") left to work on other projects.<sup id="cite_ref-57"><a href="https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/2001:_A_Space_Odyssey#cite_note-57">[56]</a></sup><sup id="cite_ref-front_projection_58-0"><a href="https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/2001:_A_Space_Odyssey#cite_note-front_projection-58">[57]</a></sup> The still photographs used as backgrounds for the *Dawn of Man* sequence were taken at the [Spitzkoppe](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Spitzkoppe "Spitzkoppe") mountains in what was then [South West Africa](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/South_West_Africa "South West Africa").<sup id="cite_ref-Chiasson_59-0"><a href="https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/2001:_A_Space_Odyssey#cite_note-Chiasson-59">[58]</a></sup><sup id="cite_ref-location_60-0"><a href="https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/2001:_A_Space_Odyssey#cite_note-location-60">[59]</a></sup>

Filming of actors was completed in September 1967,<sup id="cite_ref-61"><a href="https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/2001:_A_Space_Odyssey#cite_note-61">[60]</a></sup> and from June 1966 until March 1968, Kubrick spent most of his time working on the 205 special-effects shots in the film.<sup id="cite_ref-Gelmis_37-1"><a href="https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/2001:_A_Space_Odyssey#cite_note-Gelmis-37">[37]</a></sup> He ordered the special-effects technicians to use the painstaking process of creating all visual effects seen in the film "[in camera](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/In-camera_effect "In-camera effect")", avoiding degraded picture quality from the use of [blue screen](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Chroma_key "Chroma key") and [travelling matte](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Matte_(filmmaking) "Matte (filmmaking)") techniques. Although this technique, known as "held takes", resulted in a much better image, it meant exposed film would be stored for long periods of time between shots, sometimes as long as a year.<sup id="cite_ref-62"><a href="https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/2001:_A_Space_Odyssey#cite_note-62">[61]</a></sup> In March 1968, Kubrick finished the "pre-premiere" editing of the film, making his final cuts just days before the film's general release in April 1968.<sup id="cite_ref-Gelmis_37-2"><a href="https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/2001:_A_Space_Odyssey#cite_note-Gelmis-37">[37]</a></sup>

The film was announced in 1965 as a "Cinerama"<sup id="cite_ref-63"><a href="https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/2001:_A_Space_Odyssey#cite_note-63">[62]</a></sup> film and was photographed in [Super Panavision 70](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Super_Panavision_70 "Super Panavision 70") (which uses a 65 mm negative combined with spherical lenses to create an aspect ratio of 2.20:1). It would eventually be released in a limited "[roadshow](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Roadshow_theatrical_release "Roadshow theatrical release")" Cinerama version, then in 70 mm and 35 mm versions.<sup id="cite_ref-64"><a href="https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/2001:_A_Space_Odyssey#cite_note-64">[63]</a></sup><sup id="cite_ref-65"><a href="https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/2001:_A_Space_Odyssey#cite_note-65">[64]</a></sup> Colour processing and 35 mm release prints were done using [Technicolor](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Technicolor "Technicolor")'s dye transfer process. The 70 mm prints were made by MGM Laboratories, Inc. on [Metrocolor](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Metrocolor "Metrocolor"). The production was $4.5 million over the initial $6 million budget and 16 months behind schedule.<sup id="cite_ref-budget_66-0"><a href="https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/2001:_A_Space_Odyssey#cite_note-budget-66">[65]</a></sup> For the opening sequence involving tribes of apes, professional [mime](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mime "Mime") [Daniel Richter](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Daniel_Richter_(actor) "Daniel Richter (actor)") played the lead ape and choreographed the movements of the other man-apes, who were mostly portrayed by his mime troupe.<sup id="cite_ref-Richter_2002_55-1"><a href="https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/2001:_A_Space_Odyssey#cite_note-Richter_2002-55">[54]</a></sup> Kubrick and Clarke consulted [IBM](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/IBM "IBM") on plans for HAL, though plans to use the company's logo never materialised.<sup id="cite_ref-Chiasson_59-1"><a href="https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/2001:_A_Space_Odyssey#cite_note-Chiasson-59">[58]</a></sup>

### Post-production\[[edit](https://en.wikipedia.org/w/index.php?title=2001:_A_Space_Odyssey&action=edit&section=8 "Edit section: Post-production")\]

For cuts made after the film premiered, see the Theatrical run section below.

The film was edited before it was publicly screened, cutting out, among other things, a painting class on the lunar base that included Kubrick's daughters, additional scenes of life on the base, and Floyd buying a [bush baby](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Galago "Galago") for his daughter from a department store via videophone.<sup id="cite_ref-visual-memory_67-0"><a href="https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/2001:_A_Space_Odyssey#cite_note-visual-memory-67">[66]</a></sup> A ten-minute black-and-white opening sequence featuring interviews with scientists, including [Freeman Dyson](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Freeman_Dyson "Freeman Dyson") discussing off-Earth life,<sup id="cite_ref-68"><a href="https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/2001:_A_Space_Odyssey#cite_note-68">[67]</a></sup> was removed after an early screening for MGM executives.<sup id="cite_ref-69"><a href="https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/2001:_A_Space_Odyssey#cite_note-69">[68]</a></sup>

### Music\[[edit](https://en.wikipedia.org/w/index.php?title=2001:_A_Space_Odyssey&action=edit&section=9 "Edit section: Music")\]

From early in production, Kubrick decided that he wanted the film to be a primarily nonverbal experience<sup id="cite_ref-70"><a href="https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/2001:_A_Space_Odyssey#cite_note-70">[69]</a></sup> that did not rely on the traditional techniques of [narrative](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Narrative "Narrative") cinema, and in which music would play a vital role in evoking particular moods. About half the music in the film appears either before the first line of dialogue or after the final line. Almost no music is heard during scenes with dialogue.<sup id="cite_ref-LoBrutto_71-0"><a href="https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/2001:_A_Space_Odyssey#cite_note-LoBrutto-71">[70]</a></sup> The film is notable for its innovative use of [classical music](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Classical_music "Classical music") taken from existing commercial recordings. Most feature films, then and now, are typically accompanied by elaborate [film scores](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Film_scores "Film scores") or songs written specially for them by professional composers. In the early stages of production, Kubrick commissioned a [score for *2001*](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/2001:_A_Space_Odyssey_(score) "2001: A Space Odyssey (score)") from Hollywood composer [Alex North](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Alex_North "Alex North"), who had written the score for *[Spartacus](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Spartacus_(film) "Spartacus (film)")* and also had worked on *Dr. Strangelove*.<sup id="cite_ref-72"><a href="https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/2001:_A_Space_Odyssey#cite_note-72">[71]</a></sup><sup id="cite_ref-73"><a href="https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/2001:_A_Space_Odyssey#cite_note-73">[72]</a></sup> During post-production, Kubrick chose to abandon North's music in favour of the classical pieces which he had earlier chosen to guide North's score. North did not learn his score had been abandoned in favour of the [temporary music](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Temp_track "Temp track") pieces until he saw the film at its premiere.<sup id="cite_ref-LoBrutto_71-1"><a href="https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/2001:_A_Space_Odyssey#cite_note-LoBrutto-71">[70]</a></sup>

## Design and special effects\[[edit](https://en.wikipedia.org/w/index.php?title=2001:_A_Space_Odyssey&action=edit&section=10 "Edit section: Design and special effects")\]

### Costumes and set design\[[edit](https://en.wikipedia.org/w/index.php?title=2001:_A_Space_Odyssey&action=edit&section=11 "Edit section: Costumes and set design")\]

Kubrick involved himself in every aspect of production, even choosing the fabric for his actors' costumes,<sup id="cite_ref-74"><a href="https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/2001:_A_Space_Odyssey#cite_note-74">[73]</a></sup> and selecting notable pieces of contemporary furniture for use in the film. When Floyd exits the Space Station 5 elevator, he is greeted by an attendant seated behind a slightly modified [George Nelson](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/George_Nelson_(designer) "George Nelson (designer)") Action Office desk from [Herman Miller](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Herman_Miller_(office_equipment) "Herman Miller (office equipment)")'s 1964 "[Action Office](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Action_Office "Action Office")" series.<sup id="cite_ref-75"><a href="https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/2001:_A_Space_Odyssey#cite_note-75">[b]</a></sup><sup id="cite_ref-76"><a href="https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/2001:_A_Space_Odyssey#cite_note-76">[74]</a></sup><sup id="cite_ref-77"><a href="https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/2001:_A_Space_Odyssey#cite_note-77">[c]</a></sup> Danish designer [Arne Jacobsen](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Arne_Jacobsen "Arne Jacobsen") designed the cutlery used by the *Discovery* astronauts in the film.<sup id="cite_ref-78"><a href="https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/2001:_A_Space_Odyssey#cite_note-78">[75]</a></sup><sup id="cite_ref-79"><a href="https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/2001:_A_Space_Odyssey#cite_note-79">[76]</a></sup><sup id="cite_ref-80"><a href="https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/2001:_A_Space_Odyssey#cite_note-80">[77]</a></sup>

Other examples of modern furniture in the film are the bright red [Djinn chairs](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Djinn_chair "Djinn chair") seen prominently throughout the space station<sup id="cite_ref-Phil_Patton_81-0"><a href="https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/2001:_A_Space_Odyssey#cite_note-Phil_Patton-81">[78]</a></sup><sup id="cite_ref-Fiell2005_82-0"><a href="https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/2001:_A_Space_Odyssey#cite_note-Fiell2005-82">[79]</a></sup> and [Eero Saarinen](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Eero_Saarinen "Eero Saarinen")'s 1956 pedestal tables. [Olivier Mourgue](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Olivier_Mourgue "Olivier Mourgue"), designer of the Djinn chair, has used the connection to *2001* in his advertising; a frame from the film's space station sequence and three production stills appear on the homepage of Mourgue's website.<sup id="cite_ref-83"><a href="https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/2001:_A_Space_Odyssey#cite_note-83">[80]</a></sup> Shortly before Kubrick's death, film critic [Alexander Walker](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Alexander_Walker_(critic) "Alexander Walker (critic)") informed Kubrick of Mourgue's use of the film, joking to him "You're keeping the price up."<sup id="cite_ref-84"><a href="https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/2001:_A_Space_Odyssey#cite_note-84">[81]</a></sup> Commenting on their use in the film, Walker writes:

> Everyone recalls one early sequence in the film, the space hotel, primarily because the custom-made Olivier Mourgue furnishings, those foam-filled sofas, undulant and serpentine, are covered in scarlet fabric and are the first stabs of colour one sees. They resemble Rorschach "blots" against the pristine purity of the rest of the lobby.<sup id="cite_ref-85"><a href="https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/2001:_A_Space_Odyssey#cite_note-85">[82]</a></sup>

Detailed instructions in relatively small print for various technological devices appear at several points in the film, the most visible of which are the lengthy instructions for the zero-gravity toilet on the Aries Moon shuttle. Similar detailed instructions for replacing the [explosive bolts](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Explosive_bolts "Explosive bolts") also appear on the hatches of the EVA pods, most visibly in close-up just before Bowman's pod leaves the ship to rescue Frank Poole.<sup id="cite_ref-87"><a href="https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/2001:_A_Space_Odyssey#cite_note-87">[d]</a></sup>

The film features an extensive use of [Eurostile](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Eurostile "Eurostile") Bold Extended, [Futura](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Futura_(typeface) "Futura (typeface)") and other [sans serif](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sans_serif "Sans serif") [typefaces](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Typeface "Typeface") as design elements of the *2001* world.<sup id="cite_ref-88"><a href="https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/2001:_A_Space_Odyssey#cite_note-88">[84]</a></sup> Computer displays show high-resolution fonts, colour, and graphics that were far in advance of what most computers were capable of in the 1960s, when the film was made.<sup id="cite_ref-Agel-321-324_86-1"><a href="https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/2001:_A_Space_Odyssey#cite_note-Agel-321-324-86">[83]</a></sup>

### Design of the monolith\[[edit](https://en.wikipedia.org/w/index.php?title=2001:_A_Space_Odyssey&action=edit&section=12 "Edit section: Design of the monolith")\]

Kubrick was personally involved in the design of the monolith and its form for the film. The first design for the monolith for the *2001* film was a transparent [tetrahedral](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tetrahedron "Tetrahedron") pyramid. This was taken from the short story "The Sentinel" that the first story was based on.<sup id="cite_ref-89"><a href="https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/2001:_A_Space_Odyssey#cite_note-89">[85]</a></sup><sup id="cite_ref-90"><a href="https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/2001:_A_Space_Odyssey#cite_note-90">[86]</a></sup>

A London firm was approached by Kubrick to provide a 12-foot (3.7 m) transparent [plexiglass](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Plexiglass "Plexiglass") pyramid, and due to construction constraints they recommended a flat slab shape. Kubrick approved, but was disappointed with the glassy appearance of the transparent prop on set, leading art director Anthony Masters to suggest making the monolith's surface matte black.<sup id="cite_ref-Benson's_Book_30-3"><a href="https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/2001:_A_Space_Odyssey#cite_note-Benson's_Book-30">[30]</a></sup>

### Models\[[edit](https://en.wikipedia.org/w/index.php?title=2001:_A_Space_Odyssey&action=edit&section=13 "Edit section: Models")\]

[![](https://upload.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/thumb/2/25/Stanley_Kubrick_in_EYE_%287527338298%29.jpg/220px-Stanley_Kubrick_in_EYE_%287527338298%29.jpg)](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/File:Stanley_Kubrick_in_EYE_(7527338298).jpg)

Modern replica of the *Discovery One* spaceship model

To heighten the reality of the film, intricate models of the various spacecraft and locations were built. Their sizes ranged from about two-foot-long models of satellites and the Aries translunar shuttle up to the 55-foot (17 m)-long model of the *Discovery One* spacecraft. "In-camera" techniques were again used as much as possible to combine models and background shots together to prevent degradation of the image through duplication.<sup id="cite_ref-online_excerpt_91-0"><a href="https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/2001:_A_Space_Odyssey#cite_note-online_excerpt-91">[87]</a></sup><sup id="cite_ref-92"><a href="https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/2001:_A_Space_Odyssey#cite_note-92">[88]</a></sup>

In shots where there was no perspective change, still shots of the models were photographed and positive paper prints were made. The image of the model was cut out of the photographic print and mounted on glass and filmed on an [animation stand](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Animation_stand "Animation stand"). The undeveloped film was re-wound to film the star background with the silhouette of the model photograph acting as a matte to block out where the spaceship image was.<sup id="cite_ref-online_excerpt_91-1"><a href="https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/2001:_A_Space_Odyssey#cite_note-online_excerpt-91">[87]</a></sup>

Shots where the spacecraft had parts in motion or the perspective changed were shot by directly filming the model. For most shots the model was stationary and camera was driven along a track on a special mount, the motor of which was mechanically linked to the camera motor—making it possible to repeat camera moves and match speeds exactly. Elements of the scene were recorded on the same piece of film in separate passes to combine the lit model, stars, planets, or other spacecraft in the same shot. In moving shots of the long *Discovery One* spacecraft, in order to keep the entire model in focus (and preserve its sense of scale), the camera's aperture was stopped down for maximum depth-of-field, and each frame was exposed for several seconds.<sup id="cite_ref-Bizony_2001,_pp._112-113_93-0"><a href="https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/2001:_A_Space_Odyssey#cite_note-Bizony_2001,_pp._112-113-93">[89]</a></sup> Many matting techniques were tried to block out the stars behind the models, with film makers sometimes resorting to hand-tracing frame by frame around the image of the spacecraft ([rotoscoping](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Rotoscoping "Rotoscoping")) to create the matte.<sup id="cite_ref-online_excerpt_91-2"><a href="https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/2001:_A_Space_Odyssey#cite_note-online_excerpt-91">[87]</a></sup><sup id="cite_ref-Bizony_2001,_pp._113–117_94-0"><a href="https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/2001:_A_Space_Odyssey#cite_note-Bizony_2001,_pp._113%E2%80%93117-94">[90]</a></sup>

Some shots required exposing the film again to record previously filmed live-action shots of the people appearing in the windows of the spacecraft or structures. This was achieved by projecting the window action onto the models in a separate camera pass or, when two-dimensional photographs were used, projecting from the backside through a hole cut in the photograph.<sup id="cite_ref-Bizony_2001,_pp._112-113_93-1"><a href="https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/2001:_A_Space_Odyssey#cite_note-Bizony_2001,_pp._112-113-93">[89]</a></sup>

All of the shots required multiple takes so that some film could be developed and printed to check exposure, density, alignment of elements, and to supply footage used for other photographic effects, such as for matting.<sup id="cite_ref-online_excerpt_91-3"><a href="https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/2001:_A_Space_Odyssey#cite_note-online_excerpt-91">[87]</a></sup><sup id="cite_ref-Bizony_2001,_pp._113–117_94-1"><a href="https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/2001:_A_Space_Odyssey#cite_note-Bizony_2001,_pp._113%E2%80%93117-94">[90]</a></sup>

### Rotating sets\[[edit](https://en.wikipedia.org/w/index.php?title=2001:_A_Space_Odyssey&action=edit&section=14 "Edit section: Rotating sets")\]

[![](https://upload.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/en/thumb/f/f2/2001_CENTRIFUGE_SET.jpg/220px-2001_CENTRIFUGE_SET.jpg)](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/File:2001_CENTRIFUGE_SET.jpg)

The "centrifuge" set used for filming scenes depicting interior of the spaceship *Discovery*

For spacecraft interior shots, ostensibly containing a giant centrifuge that produces [artificial gravity](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Rotational_gravity "Rotational gravity"), Kubrick had a 30-short-ton (27 t) rotating "ferris wheel" built by [Vickers-Armstrong](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Vickers-Armstrong "Vickers-Armstrong") Engineering Group at a cost of $750,000 (equivalent to $6,600,000 in 2023<sup id="cite_ref-inflation-US_95-0"><a href="https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/2001:_A_Space_Odyssey#cite_note-inflation-US-95">[91]</a></sup>). The set was 38 feet (12 m) in diameter and 10 feet (3.0 m) wide.<sup id="cite_ref-96"><a href="https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/2001:_A_Space_Odyssey#cite_note-96">[92]</a></sup> Various scenes in the *Discovery* centrifuge were shot by securing set pieces within the wheel, then rotating it while the actor walked or ran in sync with its motion, keeping him at the bottom of the wheel as it turned. The camera could be fixed to the inside of the rotating wheel to show the actor walking completely "around" the set, or mounted in such a way that the wheel rotated independently of the stationary camera, as in the jogging scene where the camera appears to alternately precede and follow the running actor.<sup id="cite_ref-Bizony_2001_138–144_97-0"><a href="https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/2001:_A_Space_Odyssey#cite_note-Bizony_2001_138%E2%80%93144-97">[93]</a></sup>

The shots where the actors appear on opposite sides of the wheel required one of the actors to be strapped securely into place at the "top" of the wheel as it moved to allow the other actor to walk to the "bottom" of the wheel to join him. The most notable case is when Bowman enters the centrifuge from the central hub on a ladder and joins Poole, who is eating on the other side of the centrifuge. This required Gary Lockwood to be strapped into a seat while Keir Dullea walked toward him from the opposite side of the wheel as it turned with him.<sup id="cite_ref-Bizony_2001_138–144_97-1"><a href="https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/2001:_A_Space_Odyssey#cite_note-Bizony_2001_138%E2%80%93144-97">[93]</a></sup>

Another rotating set appeared in an earlier sequence on board the Aries trans-lunar shuttle. A stewardess is shown preparing in-flight meals, then carrying them into a circular walkway. Attached to the set as it rotates 180 degrees, the camera's point of view remains constant, and she appears to walk up the "side" of the circular walkway, and steps, now in an "upside-down" orientation, into a connecting hallway.<sup id="cite_ref-98"><a href="https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/2001:_A_Space_Odyssey#cite_note-98">[94]</a></sup>

### Zero-gravity effects\[[edit](https://en.wikipedia.org/w/index.php?title=2001:_A_Space_Odyssey&action=edit&section=15 "Edit section: Zero-gravity effects")\]

The realistic-looking effects of the astronauts floating weightless in space and inside the spacecraft were accomplished by suspending the actors from wires attached to the top of the set and placing the camera beneath them. The actors' bodies blocked the camera's view of the wires and appeared to float. For the shot of Poole floating into the pod's arms during Bowman's recovery of him, a stuntman on a wire portrayed the movements of an unconscious man and was shot in slow motion to enhance the illusion of drifting through space.<sup id="cite_ref-99"><a href="https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/2001:_A_Space_Odyssey#cite_note-99">[95]</a></sup> The scene showing Bowman entering the emergency airlock from the EVA pod was done similarly: an off-camera stagehand, standing on a platform, held the wire suspending Dullea above the camera positioned at the bottom of the vertically oriented airlock. At the proper moment, the stage-hand first loosened his grip on the wire, causing Dullea to fall toward the camera, then, while holding the wire firmly, jumped off the platform, causing Dullea to ascend back toward the hatch.<sup id="cite_ref-2001:A_Space_Odyssey_100-0"><a href="https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/2001:_A_Space_Odyssey#cite_note-2001:A_Space_Odyssey-100">[96]</a></sup>

The methods used were alleged to have placed stuntman [Bill Weston](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bill_Weston "Bill Weston")'s life in danger. Weston recalled that he filmed one sequence without air-holes in his suit, risking asphyxiation. "Even when the tank was feeding air into the suit, there was no place for the carbon dioxide Weston exhaled to go. So it simply built up inside, incrementally causing a heightened heart rate, rapid breathing, fatigue, clumsiness, and eventually, unconsciousness."<sup id="cite_ref-101"><a href="https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/2001:_A_Space_Odyssey#cite_note-101">[97]</a></sup> Weston said Kubrick was warned "we've got to get him back" but reportedly replied, "Damn it, we just started. Leave him up there! Leave him up there!"<sup id="cite_ref-102"><a href="https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/2001:_A_Space_Odyssey#cite_note-102">[98]</a></sup> When Weston lost consciousness, filming ceased, and he was brought down. "They brought the tower in, and I went looking for Stanley, ... I was going to shove MGM right up his ... And the thing is, Stanley had left the studio and sent Victor \[Lyndon, the associate producer\] to talk to me." Weston claimed Kubrick fled the studio for "two or three days. ... I know he didn't come in the next day, and I'm sure it wasn't the day after. Because I was going to do him."<sup id="cite_ref-103"><a href="https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/2001:_A_Space_Odyssey#cite_note-103">[99]</a></sup>

### "Star Gate" sequence\[[edit](https://en.wikipedia.org/w/index.php?title=2001:_A_Space_Odyssey&action=edit&section=16 "Edit section: "Star Gate" sequence")\]

During the "Jupiter and Beyond the Infinite" sequence, Bowman takes a trip through the "Star Gate" that involves the innovative use of slit-scan photography to create the visual effects.

The coloured lights in the Star Gate sequence were accomplished by [slit-scan photography](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Slit-scan_photography "Slit-scan photography") of thousands of high-contrast images on film, including [Op art](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Op_art "Op art") paintings, architectural drawings, [Moiré patterns](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Moir%C3%A9_pattern "Moiré pattern"), printed circuits, and electron-microscope photographs of molecular and crystal structures. Known to staff as "Manhattan Project", the shots of various nebula-like phenomena, including the expanding star field, were coloured paints and chemicals swirling in a pool-like device known as a cloud tank, shot in slow motion in a dark room.<sup id="cite_ref-104"><a href="https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/2001:_A_Space_Odyssey#cite_note-104">[100]</a></sup> The live-action landscape shots were filmed in the [Hebridean islands](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hebrides "Hebrides"), the [mountains of northern Scotland](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mountains_and_hills_of_Scotland "Mountains and hills of Scotland"), and [Monument Valley](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Monument_Valley "Monument Valley"). The colouring and negative-image effects were achieved with different colour filters in the process of making duplicate negatives in an [optical printer](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Optical_printer "Optical printer").<sup id="cite_ref-105"><a href="https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/2001:_A_Space_Odyssey#cite_note-105">[101]</a></sup>

### Visual effects\[[edit](https://en.wikipedia.org/w/index.php?title=2001:_A_Space_Odyssey&action=edit&section=17 "Edit section: Visual effects")\]

[![](https://upload.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/en/thumb/4/45/MatchCut.JPG/240px-MatchCut.JPG)](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/File:MatchCut.JPG)

A bone-club and orbiting satellite are juxtaposed in the film's famous [match cut](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Match_cut "Match cut")

> "Not one foot of this film was made with computer-generated special effects. Everything you see in this film or saw in this film was done physically or chemically, one way or the other."
> 
> — Keir Dullea (2014)<sup id="cite_ref-106"><a href="https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/2001:_A_Space_Odyssey#cite_note-106">[102]</a></sup>

*2001* contains a famous example of a [match cut](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Match_cut "Match cut"), a type of cut in which two shots are matched by action or subject matter.<sup id="cite_ref-dictionary_107-0"><a href="https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/2001:_A_Space_Odyssey#cite_note-dictionary-107">[103]</a></sup><sup id="cite_ref-Zizek_108-0"><a href="https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/2001:_A_Space_Odyssey#cite_note-Zizek-108">[104]</a></sup> After Moonwatcher uses a bone to kill another ape at the watering hole, he throws it triumphantly into the air; as the bone spins in the air, the film cuts to an orbiting satellite, marking the end of the prologue.<sup id="cite_ref-109"><a href="https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/2001:_A_Space_Odyssey#cite_note-109">[105]</a></sup> The match cut draws a connection between the two objects as exemplars of primitive and advanced tools respectively, and demonstrates humanity's technological progress since the time of early hominids.<sup id="cite_ref-filmjournal_110-0"><a href="https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/2001:_A_Space_Odyssey#cite_note-filmjournal-110">[106]</a></sup>

*2001* pioneered the use of [front projection](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Front_projection_effect "Front projection effect") with [retroreflective](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Retroreflective "Retroreflective") matting. Kubrick used the technique to produce the backdrops in the Africa scenes and the scene when astronauts walk on the Moon.<sup id="cite_ref-111"><a href="https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/2001:_A_Space_Odyssey#cite_note-111">[107]</a></sup><sup id="cite_ref-front_projection_58-1"><a href="https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/2001:_A_Space_Odyssey#cite_note-front_projection-58">[57]</a></sup>

The technique consisted of a separate scenery projector set at a right angle to the camera and a half-silvered mirror placed at an angle in front that reflected the projected image forward in line with the camera lens onto a backdrop of retroreflective material. The reflective directional screen behind the actors could reflect light from the projected image 100 times more efficiently than the foreground subject did. The lighting of the foreground subject had to be balanced with the image from the screen, so that the part of the scenery image that fell on the foreground subject was too faint to show on the finished film. The exception was the eyes of the [leopard](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Leopard "Leopard") in the "Dawn of Man" sequence, which glowed due to the projector illumination. Kubrick described this as "a happy accident".<sup id="cite_ref-Herb_A._Lightman_page2_112-0"><a href="https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/2001:_A_Space_Odyssey#cite_note-Herb_A._Lightman_page2-112">[108]</a></sup>

Front projection had been used in smaller settings before *2001*, mostly for still photography or television production, using small still images and projectors. The expansive backdrops for the African scenes required a screen 40 feet (12 m) tall and 110 feet (34 m) wide, far larger than had been used before. When the reflective material was applied to the backdrop in 100-foot (30 m) strips, variations at the seams of the strips led to visual artefacts; to solve this, the crew tore the material into smaller chunks and applied them in a random "camouflage" pattern on the backdrop. The existing projectors using 4-by-5-inch (10 cm × 13 cm) transparencies resulted in grainy images when projected that large, so the crew worked with MGM's special-effects supervisor [Tom Howard](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tom_Howard_(special_effects) "Tom Howard (special effects)") to build a custom projector using 8-by-10-inch (20 cm × 25 cm) transparencies, which required the largest water-cooled arc lamp available.<sup id="cite_ref-Herb_A._Lightman_page2_112-1"><a href="https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/2001:_A_Space_Odyssey#cite_note-Herb_A._Lightman_page2-112">[108]</a></sup> The technique was used widely in the film industry thereafter until it was replaced by blue/green screen systems in the 1990s.<sup id="cite_ref-Herb_A._Lightman_page2_112-2"><a href="https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/2001:_A_Space_Odyssey#cite_note-Herb_A._Lightman_page2-112">[108]</a></sup>

## Soundtrack\[[edit](https://en.wikipedia.org/w/index.php?title=2001:_A_Space_Odyssey&action=edit&section=18 "Edit section: Soundtrack")\]

The initial MGM soundtrack album release contained none of the material from the altered and uncredited rendition of Ligeti's *Aventures* used in the film, used a different recording of *[Also sprach Zarathustra](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Also_sprach_Zarathustra "Also sprach Zarathustra")* (performed by the [Berlin Philharmonic](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Berlin_Philharmonic "Berlin Philharmonic") conducted by [Karl Böhm](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Karl_B%C3%B6hm "Karl Böhm")) from that heard in the film, and a longer excerpt of *[Lux Aeterna](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lux_Aeterna_(Ligeti) "Lux Aeterna (Ligeti)")* than that in the film.<sup id="cite_ref-La_revolution_113-0"><a href="https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/2001:_A_Space_Odyssey#cite_note-La_revolution-113">[109]</a></sup>

In 1996, Turner Entertainment/[Rhino Records](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Rhino_Records "Rhino Records") released a new soundtrack on CD that included the film's rendition of "Aventures", the version of "Zarathustra" used in the film, and the shorter version of *Lux Aeterna* from the film. As additional "bonus tracks" at the end, the CD includes the versions of "Zarathustra" and *Lux Aeterna* on the old MGM soundtrack album, an unaltered performance of "Aventures", and a nine-minute compilation of all of HAL's dialogue.<sup id="cite_ref-La_revolution_113-1"><a href="https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/2001:_A_Space_Odyssey#cite_note-La_revolution-113">[109]</a></sup>

Alex North's unused music was first released in Telarc's issue of the main theme on *Hollywood's Greatest Hits, Vol. 2*, a compilation album by [Erich Kunzel](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Erich_Kunzel "Erich Kunzel") and the [Cincinnati Pops Orchestra](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cincinnati_Pops_Orchestra "Cincinnati Pops Orchestra"). All of the music North originally wrote was recorded commercially by his friend and colleague [Jerry Goldsmith](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Jerry_Goldsmith "Jerry Goldsmith") with the [National Philharmonic Orchestra](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/National_Philharmonic_Orchestra "National Philharmonic Orchestra") and released on [Varèse Sarabande](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Var%C3%A8se_Sarabande "Varèse Sarabande") CDs shortly after Telarc's first theme release and before North's death. Eventually, a mono mix-down of North's original recordings was released as a limited-edition CD by [Intrada Records](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Intrada_Records "Intrada Records").<sup id="cite_ref-Burt1995_114-0"><a href="https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/2001:_A_Space_Odyssey#cite_note-Burt1995-114">[110]</a></sup>

## Theatrical run and post-premiere cuts\[[edit](https://en.wikipedia.org/w/index.php?title=2001:_A_Space_Odyssey&action=edit&section=19 "Edit section: Theatrical run and post-premiere cuts")\]

[](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/File:2001_A_Space_Odyssey_(1968)_-_Trailer.webm "Play video")[](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/File:2001_A_Space_Odyssey_(1968)_-_Trailer.webm)

Original [trailer](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Trailer_(promotion) "Trailer (promotion)") for *2001: A Space Odyssey*.

The film was originally scheduled for a Christmas 1966 release, but was later delayed to spring 1967, then later October 1967.<sup id="cite_ref-115"><a href="https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/2001:_A_Space_Odyssey#cite_note-115">[111]</a></sup> The film's world [premiere](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Premiere "Premiere") was on 2 April 1968,<sup id="cite_ref-NUM_116-0"><a href="https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/2001:_A_Space_Odyssey#cite_note-NUM-116">[112]</a></sup><sup id="cite_ref-RT_117-0"><a href="https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/2001:_A_Space_Odyssey#cite_note-RT-117">[113]</a></sup> at the [Uptown Theater](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Uptown_Theater_(Washington,_D.C.) "Uptown Theater (Washington, D.C.)") in Washington, D.C.,<sup id="cite_ref-118"><a href="https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/2001:_A_Space_Odyssey#cite_note-118">[114]</a></sup> with a 160-minute cut.<sup id="cite_ref-AFI_119-0"><a href="https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/2001:_A_Space_Odyssey#cite_note-AFI-119">[115]</a></sup> It opened the next day at the [Loew's Capitol](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Loew%27s_Capitol_Theatre "Loew's Capitol Theatre") in New York and the following day at the [Warner Hollywood Theatre](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Warner_Hollywood_Theatre "Warner Hollywood Theatre") in Los Angeles.<sup id="cite_ref-AFI_119-1"><a href="https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/2001:_A_Space_Odyssey#cite_note-AFI-119">[115]</a></sup> The original version was also shown in Boston.

Kubrick and editor Ray Lovejoy edited the film between 5 April and 9, 1968. Kubrick's rationale for trimming the film was to tighten the narrative. Reviews suggested the film suffered from its departure from traditional cinematic storytelling.<sup id="cite_ref-Frederick_120-0"><a href="https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/2001:_A_Space_Odyssey#cite_note-Frederick-120">[116]</a></sup> Kubrick said, "I didn't believe that the trims made a critical difference. ... The people who like it like it no matter what its length, and the same holds true for the people who hate it."<sup id="cite_ref-visual-memory_67-1"><a href="https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/2001:_A_Space_Odyssey#cite_note-visual-memory-67">[66]</a></sup> The cut footage is reported as being 19<sup id="cite_ref-trim_121-0"><a href="https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/2001:_A_Space_Odyssey#cite_note-trim-121">[117]</a></sup><sup id="cite_ref-122"><a href="https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/2001:_A_Space_Odyssey#cite_note-122">[118]</a></sup> or 17<sup id="cite_ref-WarnersCut_123-0"><a href="https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/2001:_A_Space_Odyssey#cite_note-WarnersCut-123">[119]</a></sup> minutes long. It includes scenes revealing details about life on *Discovery*: additional space walks, Bowman retrieving a spare part from an octagonal corridor, elements from the Poole murder sequence—including space-walk preparation and HAL turning off radio contact with Poole—and a close-up of Bowman picking up a slipper during his walk in the alien room.<sup id="cite_ref-visual-memory_67-2"><a href="https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/2001:_A_Space_Odyssey#cite_note-visual-memory-67">[66]</a></sup> Jerome Agel describes the cut scenes as comprising "Dawn of Man, Orion, Poole exercising in the centrifuge, and Poole's pod exiting from *Discovery*."<sup id="cite_ref-auto3_124-0"><a href="https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/2001:_A_Space_Odyssey#cite_note-auto3-124">[120]</a></sup> The new cut was approximately 139 minutes long.<sup id="cite_ref-nyt_review_2-1"><a href="https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/2001:_A_Space_Odyssey#cite_note-nyt_review-2">[2]</a></sup>

According to his brother-in-law, [Jan Harlan](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Jan_Harlan "Jan Harlan"), Kubrick was adamant that the trims were never to be seen and had the negatives, which he had kept in his garage, burned shortly before his death. This was confirmed by former Kubrick assistant [Leon Vitali](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Leon_Vitali "Leon Vitali"): "I'll tell you right now, okay, on *Clockwork Orange*, *The Shining*, *Barry Lyndon*, some little parts of *2001*, we had thousands of cans of negative outtakes and print, which we had stored in an area at his house where we worked out of, which he personally supervised the loading of it to a truck and then I went down to a big industrial waste lot and burned it. That's what he wanted."<sup id="cite_ref-125"><a href="https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/2001:_A_Space_Odyssey#cite_note-125">[121]</a></sup> However, in December 2010, Douglas Trumbull, the film's visual effects supervisor, announced that [Warner Bros.](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Warner_Bros. "Warner Bros.") had found 17 minutes of lost footage from the post-premiere cuts, "perfectly preserved", in a Kansas [salt mine](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Salt_mine "Salt mine") vault used by Warners for storage.<sup id="cite_ref-126"><a href="https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/2001:_A_Space_Odyssey#cite_note-126">[122]</a></sup><sup id="cite_ref-auto3_124-1"><a href="https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/2001:_A_Space_Odyssey#cite_note-auto3-124">[120]</a></sup><sup id="cite_ref-WarnersCut_123-1"><a href="https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/2001:_A_Space_Odyssey#cite_note-WarnersCut-123">[119]</a></sup> No plans have been announced for the rediscovered footage.<sup id="cite_ref-127"><a href="https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/2001:_A_Space_Odyssey#cite_note-127">[123]</a></sup>

The revised version was ready for the expansion of the roadshow release to four other U.S. cities (Chicago, Denver, Detroit and Houston), on 10 April 1968, and internationally in five cities the following day,<sup id="cite_ref-auto3_124-2"><a href="https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/2001:_A_Space_Odyssey#cite_note-auto3-124">[120]</a></sup><sup id="cite_ref-underview_128-0"><a href="https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/2001:_A_Space_Odyssey#cite_note-underview-128">[124]</a></sup> where the shortened version was shown in [70mm](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/70mm "70mm") format in the 2.21:1 [aspect ratio](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Aspect_ratio_(image) "Aspect ratio (image)") and used a six-track stereo magnetic soundtrack.<sup id="cite_ref-auto3_124-3"><a href="https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/2001:_A_Space_Odyssey#cite_note-auto3-124">[120]</a></sup>

By the end of May, the film had opened in 22 cities in the United States and Canada and in another 36 in June.<sup id="cite_ref-nine_129-0"><a href="https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/2001:_A_Space_Odyssey#cite_note-nine-129">[125]</a></sup> The general release of the film in its 35 mm [anamorphic](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Anamorphic "Anamorphic") format took place in autumn 1968 and used either a four-track magnetic stereo soundtrack or an optical monaural one.<sup id="cite_ref-in70mm_130-0"><a href="https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/2001:_A_Space_Odyssey#cite_note-in70mm-130">[126]</a></sup>

The original 70-millimetre release, like many Super Panavision 70 films of the era such as *[Grand Prix](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Grand_Prix_(1966_film) "Grand Prix (1966 film)")*, was advertised as being in "Cinerama" in cinemas equipped with special projection optics and a deeply curved screen. In standard cinemas, the film was identified as a 70-millimetre production. The original release of *2001: A Space Odyssey* in 70-millimetre Cinerama with six-track sound played continually for more than a year in several venues, and for 103 weeks in Los Angeles.<sup id="cite_ref-in70mm_130-1"><a href="https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/2001:_A_Space_Odyssey#cite_note-in70mm-130">[126]</a></sup>

As was typical of most films of the era released both as a "roadshow" (in Cinerama format in the case of *2001*) and general release (in 70-millimetre in the case of *2001*), the entrance music, intermission music (and intermission altogether), and postcredit exit music were cut from most prints of the latter version, although these have been restored to most DVD releases.<sup id="cite_ref-131"><a href="https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/2001:_A_Space_Odyssey#cite_note-131">[127]</a></sup><sup id="cite_ref-132"><a href="https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/2001:_A_Space_Odyssey#cite_note-132">[128]</a></sup>

## Reception and legacy\[[edit](https://en.wikipedia.org/w/index.php?title=2001:_A_Space_Odyssey&action=edit&section=20 "Edit section: Reception and legacy")\]

### Box office\[[edit](https://en.wikipedia.org/w/index.php?title=2001:_A_Space_Odyssey&action=edit&section=21 "Edit section: Box office")\]

In its first nine weeks from 22 locations, it grossed $2 million—equivalent to $20.15 million in 2023—in the United States and Canada.<sup id="cite_ref-nine_129-1"><a href="https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/2001:_A_Space_Odyssey#cite_note-nine-129">[125]</a></sup> The film earned $8.5 million in theatrical [gross rentals](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Gross_rental "Gross rental") from roadshow engagements throughout 1968,<sup id="cite_ref-Hall_133-0"><a href="https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/2001:_A_Space_Odyssey#cite_note-Hall-133">[129]</a></sup><sup id="cite_ref-134"><a href="https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/2001:_A_Space_Odyssey#cite_note-134">[130]</a></sup> contributing to North American rentals of $16.4 million and worldwide rentals of $21.9 million during its original release.<sup id="cite_ref-135"><a href="https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/2001:_A_Space_Odyssey#cite_note-135">[131]</a></sup> The film's high costs, of approximately $10.5 million,<sup id="cite_ref-NUM_116-1"><a href="https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/2001:_A_Space_Odyssey#cite_note-NUM-116">[112]</a></sup><sup id="cite_ref-budget_66-1"><a href="https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/2001:_A_Space_Odyssey#cite_note-budget-66">[65]</a></sup> meant that the initial returns from the 1968 release left it $800,000 in the red; but the successful re-release in 1971 made it profitable.<sup id="cite_ref-Block_(2010)_136-0"><a href="https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/2001:_A_Space_Odyssey#cite_note-Block_(2010)-136">[132]</a></sup><sup id="cite_ref-Miller_Camera_137-0"><a href="https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/2001:_A_Space_Odyssey#cite_note-Miller_Camera-137">[133]</a></sup><sup id="cite_ref-138"><a href="https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/2001:_A_Space_Odyssey#cite_note-138">[134]</a></sup> By June 1974, the film had rentals from the United States and Canada of $20.3 million (gross of $58 million)<sup id="cite_ref-Block_(2010)_136-1"><a href="https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/2001:_A_Space_Odyssey#cite_note-Block_(2010)-136">[132]</a></sup> and international rentals of $7.5 million.<sup id="cite_ref-reissue_139-0"><a href="https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/2001:_A_Space_Odyssey#cite_note-reissue-139">[135]</a></sup> The film had a reissue on a test basis on 24 July 1974 at the [Cinerama Dome](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cinerama_Dome "Cinerama Dome") in Los Angeles and grossed $53,000 in its first week, which led to an expanded reissue.<sup id="cite_ref-reissue_139-1"><a href="https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/2001:_A_Space_Odyssey#cite_note-reissue-139">[135]</a></sup> Further re-releases followed, giving a cumulative gross of over $60 million in the United States and Canada.<sup id="cite_ref-BOM_140-0"><a href="https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/2001:_A_Space_Odyssey#cite_note-BOM-140">[136]</a></sup> Taking its re-releases into account, it is the highest-grossing film of 1968 in the United States and Canada.<sup id="cite_ref-141"><a href="https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/2001:_A_Space_Odyssey#cite_note-141">[137]</a></sup> Worldwide, it has grossed $146 million across all releases,<sup id="cite_ref-143"><a href="https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/2001:_A_Space_Odyssey#cite_note-143">[e]</a></sup> although some estimates place the gross higher, at over $190 million.<sup id="cite_ref-Miller_Corner_144-0"><a href="https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/2001:_A_Space_Odyssey#cite_note-Miller_Corner-144">[139]</a></sup>

### Critical response\[[edit](https://en.wikipedia.org/w/index.php?title=2001:_A_Space_Odyssey&action=edit&section=22 "Edit section: Critical response")\]

[![](https://upload.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/thumb/3/3d/Walt_Crowley_illustration_for_2001_A_Space_Odyssey.jpg/220px-Walt_Crowley_illustration_for_2001_A_Space_Odyssey.jpg)](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/File:Walt_Crowley_illustration_for_2001_A_Space_Odyssey.jpg)

Seattle writer [Walt Crowley](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Walt_Crowley "Walt Crowley") drew this cartoon to illustrate his favorable review in the Seattle underground paper *[Helix](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Helix_(newspaper) "Helix (newspaper)")*, in which he accused mainstream critics of "approach\[ing\] [Kandinsky](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Wassily_Kandinsky "Wassily Kandinsky") with a [Norman Rockwell](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Norman_Rockwell "Norman Rockwell") mentality."<sup id="cite_ref-145"><a href="https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/2001:_A_Space_Odyssey#cite_note-145">[140]</a></sup>

*2001* polarised critical opinion, receiving both praise and derision, with many New York-based critics being especially harsh. Kubrick called them "dogmatically atheistic and materialistic and earthbound".<sup id="cite_ref-THR_146-0"><a href="https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/2001:_A_Space_Odyssey#cite_note-THR-146">[141]</a></sup> Some critics viewed the original 161-minute cut shown at premieres in [Washington D.C.](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Washington,_D.C. "Washington, D.C."), New York, and Los Angeles.<sup id="cite_ref-147"><a href="https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/2001:_A_Space_Odyssey#cite_note-147">[142]</a></sup> Keir Dullea says that during the New York premiere, 250 people walked out; in L.A., [Rock Hudson](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Rock_Hudson "Rock Hudson") not only left early but "was heard to mutter, 'What is this bullshit?'"<sup id="cite_ref-THR_146-1"><a href="https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/2001:_A_Space_Odyssey#cite_note-THR-146">[141]</a></sup> "Will someone tell me what the hell this is about?"<sup id="cite_ref-148"><a href="https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/2001:_A_Space_Odyssey#cite_note-148">[143]</a></sup> "But a few months into the release, they realised a lot of people were watching it while smoking funny cigarettes. Someone in San Francisco even ran right through the screen screaming: 'It's God!' So they came up with a new poster that said: '2001 – the ultimate trip!'"<sup id="cite_ref-149"><a href="https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/2001:_A_Space_Odyssey#cite_note-149">[144]</a></sup>

In *[The New Yorker](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/The_New_Yorker "The New Yorker")*, [Penelope Gilliatt](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Penelope_Gilliatt "Penelope Gilliatt") said it was "some kind of great film, and an unforgettable endeavor ... The film is hypnotically entertaining, and it is funny without once being gaggy, but it is also rather harrowing."<sup id="cite_ref-Gilliatt_150-0"><a href="https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/2001:_A_Space_Odyssey#cite_note-Gilliatt-150">[145]</a></sup> [Charles Champlin](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Charles_Champlin "Charles Champlin") of the *[Los Angeles Times](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Los_Angeles_Times "Los Angeles Times")* wrote that it was "the picture that science fiction fans of every age and in every corner of the world have prayed (sometimes forlornly) that the industry might some day give them. It is an ultimate statement of the science fiction film, an awesome realization of the spatial future ... it is a milestone, a landmark for a spacemark, in the art of film."<sup id="cite_ref-Champlin_151-0"><a href="https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/2001:_A_Space_Odyssey#cite_note-Champlin-151">[146]</a></sup> Louise Sweeney of *[The Christian Science Monitor](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/The_Christian_Science_Monitor "The Christian Science Monitor")* felt that *2001* was "a brilliant intergalactic satire on modern technology. It's also a dazzling 160-minute tour on the Kubrick filmship through the universe out there beyond our earth."<sup id="cite_ref-Sweeney_152-0"><a href="https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/2001:_A_Space_Odyssey#cite_note-Sweeney-152">[147]</a></sup> [Philip French](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Philip_French "Philip French") wrote that the film was "perhaps the first multi-million-dollar supercolossal movie since [D.W. Griffith](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/D.W._Griffith "D.W. Griffith")'s *[Intolerance](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Intolerance_(film) "Intolerance (film)")* fifty years ago which can be regarded as the work of one man ... *Space Odyssey* is important as the high-water mark of science-fiction movie making, or at least of the genre's futuristic branch."<sup id="cite_ref-French_153-0"><a href="https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/2001:_A_Space_Odyssey#cite_note-French-153">[148]</a></sup> *[The Boston Globe](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/The_Boston_Globe "The Boston Globe")*'s review called it "the world's most extraordinary film. Nothing like it has ever been shown in Boston before or, for that matter, anywhere ... The film is as exciting as the discovery of a new dimension in life."<sup id="cite_ref-Adams_154-0"><a href="https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/2001:_A_Space_Odyssey#cite_note-Adams-154">[149]</a></sup> [Roger Ebert](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Roger_Ebert "Roger Ebert") gave the film four stars in his original review, saying the film "succeeds magnificently on a cosmic scale".<sup id="cite_ref-Ebert_51-1"><a href="https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/2001:_A_Space_Odyssey#cite_note-Ebert-51">[50]</a></sup> He later put it on his Top 10 list for *[Sight & Sound](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sight_%26_Sound "Sight & Sound")*.<sup id="cite_ref-155"><a href="https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/2001:_A_Space_Odyssey#cite_note-155">[150]</a></sup> *[Time](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Time_(magazine) "Time (magazine)")* provided at least seven different mini-reviews in various issues in 1968, each slightly more positive than the preceding one; in the final review of 27 December 1968, the magazine called *2001* "an epic film about the history and future of mankind, brilliantly directed by Stanley Kubrick. The special effects are mindblowing."<sup id="cite_ref-Unknown_156-0"><a href="https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/2001:_A_Space_Odyssey#cite_note-Unknown-156">[151]</a></sup>

Others were unimpressed. [Pauline Kael](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pauline_Kael "Pauline Kael") called it "a monumentally unimaginative movie".<sup id="cite_ref-157"><a href="https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/2001:_A_Space_Odyssey#cite_note-157">[152]</a></sup> [Stanley Kauffmann](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Stanley_Kauffmann "Stanley Kauffmann") of *[The New Republic](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/The_New_Republic "The New Republic")* described it as "a film that is so dull, it even dulls our interest in the technical ingenuity for the sake of which Kubrick has allowed it to become dull".<sup id="cite_ref-Kauffman_Lost_158-0"><a href="https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/2001:_A_Space_Odyssey#cite_note-Kauffman_Lost-158">[153]</a></sup> The Soviet director [Andrei Tarkovsky](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Andrei_Tarkovsky "Andrei Tarkovsky") found the film to be an inadequate addition to the science fiction genre of [filmmaking](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Filmmaking "Filmmaking").<sup id="cite_ref-Benson's_Book_30-4"><a href="https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/2001:_A_Space_Odyssey#cite_note-Benson's_Book-30">[30]</a></sup> [Renata Adler](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Renata_Adler "Renata Adler") of *[The New York Times](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/The_New_York_Times "The New York Times")* wrote that it was "somewhere between hypnotic and immensely boring".<sup id="cite_ref-Adler_159-0"><a href="https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/2001:_A_Space_Odyssey#cite_note-Adler-159">[154]</a></sup> [*Variety*](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Variety_(magazine) "Variety (magazine)")'s Robert B. Frederick ('Robe') believed it was a "\[b\]ig, beautiful, but plodding sci-fi epic ... A major achievement in cinematography and special effects, *2001* lacks dramatic appeal to a large degree and only conveys suspense after the halfway mark."<sup id="cite_ref-Frederick_120-1"><a href="https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/2001:_A_Space_Odyssey#cite_note-Frederick-120">[116]</a></sup> [Andrew Sarris](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Andrew_Sarris "Andrew Sarris") called it "one of the grimmest films I have ever seen in my life ... *2001* is a disaster because it is much too abstract to make its abstract points."<sup id="cite_ref-Sarris_160-0"><a href="https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/2001:_A_Space_Odyssey#cite_note-Sarris-160">[155]</a></sup> (Sarris reversed his opinion upon a second viewing, and declared, "*2001* is indeed a major work by a major artist."<sup id="cite_ref-161"><a href="https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/2001:_A_Space_Odyssey#cite_note-161">[156]</a></sup>) [John Simon](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/John_Simon_(critic) "John Simon (critic)") felt it was "a regrettable failure, although not a total one. This film is fascinating when it concentrates on apes or machines ... and dreadful when it deals with the in-betweens: humans ... *2001*, for all its lively visual and mechanical spectacle, is a kind of space-*Spartacus* and, more pretentious still, a [shaggy God story](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Shaggy_God_story "Shaggy God story")."<sup id="cite_ref-Simon_162-0"><a href="https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/2001:_A_Space_Odyssey#cite_note-Simon-162">[157]</a></sup> Historian [Arthur M. Schlesinger, Jr.](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Arthur_M._Schlesinger,_Jr. "Arthur M. Schlesinger, Jr.") deemed the film "morally pretentious, intellectually obscure and inordinately long ... a film out of control".<sup id="cite_ref-myth_163-0"><a href="https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/2001:_A_Space_Odyssey#cite_note-myth-163">[158]</a></sup> In a 2001 review, the BBC said that its slow pacing often alienates modern audiences more than it did upon its initial release.<sup id="cite_ref-164"><a href="https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/2001:_A_Space_Odyssey#cite_note-164">[159]</a></sup>

On review aggregator [Rotten Tomatoes](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Rotten_Tomatoes "Rotten Tomatoes"), the film has a rating of 92% based on 118 reviews, with an average rating of 9.2/10. The website's critical consensus reads: "One of the most influential of all sci-fi films – and one of the most controversial – Stanley Kubrick's *2001* is a delicate, poetic meditation on the ingenuity – and folly – of mankind."<sup id="cite_ref-RT_117-1"><a href="https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/2001:_A_Space_Odyssey#cite_note-RT-117">[113]</a></sup> Review aggregator [Metacritic](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Metacritic "Metacritic"), which uses a [weighted average](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Weighted_average "Weighted average"), has assigned the film a score of 84 out of 100, based on 25 critic reviews.<sup id="cite_ref-165"><a href="https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/2001:_A_Space_Odyssey#cite_note-165">[160]</a></sup> *2001* was the only science fiction film to make *Sight & Sound*'s [2012 list of the ten best films](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/The_Sight_%26_Sound_Greatest_Films_of_All_Time_2012 "The Sight & Sound Greatest Films of All Time 2012"),<sup id="cite_ref-bfi2012_166-0"><a href="https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/2001:_A_Space_Odyssey#cite_note-bfi2012-166">[161]</a></sup> and tops the [Online Film Critics Society](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Online_Film_Critics_Society "Online Film Critics Society") list of greatest science fiction films of all time.<sup id="cite_ref-167"><a href="https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/2001:_A_Space_Odyssey#cite_note-167">[162]</a></sup> In 2012, the [Motion Picture Editors Guild](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Motion_Picture_Editors_Guild "Motion Picture Editors Guild") listed it as the 19th best-edited film of all time based on a survey of its membership.<sup id="cite_ref-168"><a href="https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/2001:_A_Space_Odyssey#cite_note-168">[163]</a></sup> Other lists that include the film are *[50 Films to See Before You Die](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/50_Films_to_See_Before_You_Die "50 Films to See Before You Die")* (#6), *The Village Voice* 100 Best Films of the 20th century (#11), and Roger Ebert's Top Ten (1968) (#2). In 1995, the [Vatican](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Vatican_City "Vatican City") named it one of the 45 best films ever made (and included it in a sub-list of the "Top Ten Art Movies" of all time.)<sup id="cite_ref-169"><a href="https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/2001:_A_Space_Odyssey#cite_note-169">[164]</a></sup> In 1998, *[Time Out](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Time_Out_(magazine) "Time Out (magazine)")* conducted a reader's poll and *2001: A Space Odyssey* was voted as ninth on the list of "greatest films of all time".<sup id="cite_ref-170"><a href="https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/2001:_A_Space_Odyssey#cite_note-170">[165]</a></sup> *[Entertainment Weekly](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Entertainment_Weekly "Entertainment Weekly")* voted it no. 26 on their list of *100 Greatest Movies of All Time*.<sup id="cite_ref-171"><a href="https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/2001:_A_Space_Odyssey#cite_note-171">[166]</a></sup> In 2017, *[Empire](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Empire_(film_magazine) "Empire (film magazine)")* magazine's readers' poll ranked it 21st on its list of "The 100 Greatest Movies".<sup id="cite_ref-172"><a href="https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/2001:_A_Space_Odyssey#cite_note-172">[167]</a></sup> In the *Sight & Sound* poll of 480 directors published in December 2022, *2001: A Space Odyssey* was voted as the Greatest Film of All Time, ahead of *[Citizen Kane](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Citizen_Kane "Citizen Kane")* and *[The Godfather](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/The_Godfather "The Godfather")*.<sup id="cite_ref-173"><a href="https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/2001:_A_Space_Odyssey#cite_note-173">[168]</a></sup><sup id="cite_ref-S&amp;S2022_174-0"><a href="https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/2001:_A_Space_Odyssey#cite_note-S&amp;S2022-174">[169]</a></sup>

### Science fiction writers\[[edit](https://en.wikipedia.org/w/index.php?title=2001:_A_Space_Odyssey&action=edit&section=23 "Edit section: Science fiction writers")\]

The film won the [Hugo Award for Best Dramatic Presentation](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hugo_Award_for_Best_Dramatic_Presentation "Hugo Award for Best Dramatic Presentation"), as voted by science fiction fans and published science-fiction writers.<sup id="cite_ref-hugo_175-0"><a href="https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/2001:_A_Space_Odyssey#cite_note-hugo-175">[170]</a></sup> [Ray Bradbury](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ray_Bradbury "Ray Bradbury") praised the film's photography but disliked the banality of most of the dialogue and believed that the audience does not care when Poole dies.<sup id="cite_ref-176"><a href="https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/2001:_A_Space_Odyssey#cite_note-176">[171]</a></sup> Both he and [Lester del Rey](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lester_del_Rey "Lester del Rey") disliked the film's feeling of sterility and blandness in the human encounters amidst the technological wonders, while both praised the pictorial element of the film. Reporting that "half the audience had left by intermission", Del Rey described the film as dull, confusing, and boring ("the first of the [New Wave](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/New_Wave_(science_fiction) "New Wave (science fiction)")\-Thing movies, with the usual empty symbols"), predicting "\[i\]t will probably be a box-office disaster, too, and thus set major science-fiction movie making back another ten years".<sup id="cite_ref-delrey196807_177-0"><a href="https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/2001:_A_Space_Odyssey#cite_note-delrey196807-177">[172]</a></sup>

[Samuel R. Delany](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Samuel_R._Delany "Samuel R. Delany") was impressed by how the film undercuts the audience's normal sense of space and orientation in several ways. Like Bradbury, Delany noticed the banality of the dialogue (he stated that characters say nothing meaningful), but regarded this as a dramatic strength, a prelude to the rebirth at the conclusion of the film.<sup id="cite_ref-178"><a href="https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/2001:_A_Space_Odyssey#cite_note-178">[173]</a></sup>

Without analysing the film in detail, [Isaac Asimov](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Isaac_Asimov "Isaac Asimov") spoke well of it in his autobiography and other essays. [James P. Hogan](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/James_P._Hogan_(writer) "James P. Hogan (writer)") liked the film but complained that the ending did not make any sense to him, leading to a bet about whether he could write something better: "I stole Arthur's plot idea shamelessly and produced *[Inherit the Stars](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Inherit_the_Stars "Inherit the Stars")*."<sup id="cite_ref-179"><a href="https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/2001:_A_Space_Odyssey#cite_note-179">[174]</a></sup>

## Interpretations\[[edit](https://en.wikipedia.org/w/index.php?title=2001:_A_Space_Odyssey&action=edit&section=25 "Edit section: Interpretations")\]

Since its premiere, *2001: A Space Odyssey* has been analysed and interpreted by professional critics and theorists, amateur writers, and science fiction fans. In his [monograph](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Monograph "Monograph") for BFI analysing the film, Peter Krämer summarised the diverse interpretations as ranging from those who saw it as darkly [apocalyptic](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Apocalypse "Apocalypse") in tone to those who saw it as an optimistic reappraisal of the hopes of mankind and humanity.<sup id="cite_ref-auto1_197-0"><a href="https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/2001:_A_Space_Odyssey#cite_note-auto1-197">[192]</a></sup> Questions about *2001* range from uncertainty about its implications for humanity's origins and destiny in the universe<sup id="cite_ref-198"><a href="https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/2001:_A_Space_Odyssey#cite_note-198">[193]</a></sup> to interpreting elements of the film's more enigmatic scenes, such as the meaning of the monolith, or the fate of astronaut David Bowman. There are also simpler and more mundane questions about the plot, in particular the causes of HAL's breakdown (explained in earlier drafts but kept mysterious in the film).<sup id="cite_ref-199"><a href="https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/2001:_A_Space_Odyssey#cite_note-199">[194]</a></sup><sup id="cite_ref-SF-20180706_44-1"><a href="https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/2001:_A_Space_Odyssey#cite_note-SF-20180706-44">[44]</a></sup><sup id="cite_ref-SYFY-20180705_200-0"><a href="https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/2001:_A_Space_Odyssey#cite_note-SYFY-20180705-200">[195]</a></sup><sup id="cite_ref-NYT-20180418_201-0"><a href="https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/2001:_A_Space_Odyssey#cite_note-NYT-20180418-201">[196]</a></sup>

### Audiences vs. critics\[[edit](https://en.wikipedia.org/w/index.php?title=2001:_A_Space_Odyssey&action=edit&section=26 "Edit section: Audiences vs. critics")\]

A spectrum of diverse interpretative opinions would form after the film's release, appearing to divide theatre audiences from the opinions of critics. Krämer writes: "Many people sent letters to Kubrick to tell him about their responses to *2001*, most of them regarding the film—in particular the ending—as an optimistic statement about humanity, which is seen to be born and reborn. The film's reviewers and academic critics, by contrast, have tended to understand the film as a pessimistic account of human nature and humanity's future. The most extreme of these interpretations state that the foetus floating above the Earth will destroy it."<sup id="cite_ref-202"><a href="https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/2001:_A_Space_Odyssey#cite_note-202">[197]</a></sup>

[![](https://upload.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/thumb/f/fd/Dr._Strangelove_-_Riding_the_Bomb.png/220px-Dr._Strangelove_-_Riding_the_Bomb.png)](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/File:Dr._Strangelove_-_Riding_the_Bomb.png)

Closing scene of *Dr. Strangelove* and Kubrick's sardonic fulfilment of a nuclear nightmare

Some of the critics' cataclysmic interpretations were informed by Kubrick's prior direction of the [Cold War](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cold_War "Cold War") film *Dr. Strangelove*, immediately before *2001*, which resulted in dark speculation about the nuclear weapons orbiting the Earth in *2001*. These interpretations were challenged by Clarke, who said: "Many readers have interpreted the last paragraph of the book to mean that he (the foetus) destroyed Earth, perhaps for the purpose of creating a new Heaven. This idea never occurred to me; it seems clear that he triggered the orbiting nuclear bombs harmlessly ...".<sup id="cite_ref-auto1_197-1"><a href="https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/2001:_A_Space_Odyssey#cite_note-auto1-197">[192]</a></sup> In response to Jeremy Bernstein's dark interpretation of the film's ending, Kubrick said: "The book does not end with the destruction of the Earth."<sup id="cite_ref-auto1_197-2"><a href="https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/2001:_A_Space_Odyssey#cite_note-auto1-197">[192]</a></sup>

Regarding the film as a whole, Kubrick encouraged people to make their own interpretations and refused to offer an explanation of "what really happened". In a 1968 interview with *Playboy* magazine, he said:

> You're free to speculate as you wish about the philosophical and [allegorical](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Allegory "Allegory") meaning of the film—and such speculation is one indication that it has succeeded in gripping the audience at a deep level—but I don't want to spell out a verbal road map for *2001* that every viewer will feel obligated to pursue or else fear he's missed the point.<sup id="cite_ref-Playboy_43-1"><a href="https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/2001:_A_Space_Odyssey#cite_note-Playboy-43">[43]</a></sup>

In a subsequent discussion of the film with Joseph Gelmis, Kubrick said his main aim was to avoid "intellectual verbalization" and reach "the viewer's subconscious". But he said he did not strive for ambiguity—it was simply an inevitable outcome of making the film nonverbal. Still, he acknowledged this ambiguity was an invaluable asset to the film. He was willing then to give a fairly straightforward explanation of the plot on what he called the "simplest level", but unwilling to discuss the film's metaphysical interpretation, which he felt should be left up to viewers.<sup id="cite_ref-203"><a href="https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/2001:_A_Space_Odyssey#cite_note-203">[198]</a></sup>

### Meaning of the monolith\[[edit](https://en.wikipedia.org/w/index.php?title=2001:_A_Space_Odyssey&action=edit&section=27 "Edit section: Meaning of the monolith")\]

For some readers, Clarke's more straightforward novel based on the script is key to interpreting the film. The novel explicitly identifies the monolith as a tool created by an alien race who have been through many stages of evolution, moving from organic form to biomechanical, and finally achieving a state of pure energy. These aliens travel the cosmos assisting lesser species to take evolutionary steps. Conversely, film critic [Penelope Houston](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Penelope_Houston_(film_critic) "Penelope Houston (film critic)") wrote in 1971 that because the novel differs in many key aspects from the film, it perhaps should not be regarded as the skeleton key to unlock it.<sup id="cite_ref-204"><a href="https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/2001:_A_Space_Odyssey#cite_note-204">[199]</a></sup>

[![](https://upload.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/en/thumb/5/53/African_monolith_2001.jpg/220px-African_monolith_2001.jpg)](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/File:African_monolith_2001.jpg)

Multiple interpretations of the meaning of the monolith have been examined in the critical reception of the film

Carolyn Geduld writes that what "structurally unites all four episodes of the film" is the monolith, the film's largest and most unresolvable enigma.<sup id="cite_ref-auto2_205-0"><a href="https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/2001:_A_Space_Odyssey#cite_note-auto2-205">[200]</a></sup> Vincent LoBrutto's biography of Kubrick says that for many, Clarke's novel supplements the understanding of the monolith which is more ambiguously depicted in the film.<sup id="cite_ref-206"><a href="https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/2001:_A_Space_Odyssey#cite_note-206">[201]</a></sup> Similarly, Geduld observes that "the monolith ... has a very simple explanation in Clarke's novel", though she later asserts that even the novel does not fully explain the ending.<sup id="cite_ref-auto2_205-1"><a href="https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/2001:_A_Space_Odyssey#cite_note-auto2-205">[200]</a></sup>

Bob McClay's *[Rolling Stone](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Rolling_Stone "Rolling Stone")* review describes a parallel between the monolith's first appearance in which tool usage is imparted to the apes (thus 'beginning' mankind) and the completion of "another evolution" in the fourth and final encounter<sup id="cite_ref-207"><a href="https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/2001:_A_Space_Odyssey#cite_note-207">[202]</a></sup> with the monolith. In a similar vein, Tim Dirks ends his synopsis saying "\[t\]he cyclical evolution from ape to man to spaceman to angel-starchild-superman is complete."<sup id="cite_ref-filmsite.org_208-0"><a href="https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/2001:_A_Space_Odyssey#cite_note-filmsite.org-208">[203]</a></sup>

Humanity's first and second encounters with the monolith have visual elements in common; both the apes, and later the astronauts, touch it gingerly with their hands, and both sequences conclude with near-identical images of the Sun appearing directly over it (the first with a crescent moon adjacent to it in the sky, the second with a near-identical crescent Earth in the same position), echoing the Sun–Earth–Moon [alignment](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Syzygy_(astronomy) "Syzygy (astronomy)") seen at the very beginning of the film.<sup id="cite_ref-209"><a href="https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/2001:_A_Space_Odyssey#cite_note-209">[204]</a></sup> The second encounter also suggests the triggering of the monolith's radio signal to Jupiter by the presence of humans, echoing the premise of Clarke's source story "The Sentinel".<sup id="cite_ref-210"><a href="https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/2001:_A_Space_Odyssey#cite_note-210">[205]</a></sup>

The monolith is the subject of the film's final line of dialogue (spoken at the end of the "Jupiter Mission" segment): "Its origin and purpose still a total mystery." Reviewers McClay and Roger Ebert wrote that the monolith is the main element of mystery in the film; Ebert described "the shock of the monolith's straight edges and square corners among the weathered rocks", and the apes warily circling it as prefiguring man reaching "for the stars".<sup id="cite_ref-Ebert_51-2"><a href="https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/2001:_A_Space_Odyssey#cite_note-Ebert-51">[50]</a></sup> Patrick Webster suggests the final line relates to how the film should be approached as a whole: "The line appends not merely to the discovery of the monolith on the Moon, but to our understanding of the film in the light of the ultimate questions it raises about the mystery of the universe."<sup id="cite_ref-211"><a href="https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/2001:_A_Space_Odyssey#cite_note-211">[206]</a></sup>

According to other scholars, "the monolith is a representation of the actual wideframe cinema screen, rotated 90 degrees ... a symbolic cinema screen".<sup id="cite_ref-212"><a href="https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/2001:_A_Space_Odyssey#cite_note-212">[207]</a></sup> "It is at once a screen and the opposite of a screen, since its black surface only absorbs, and sends nothing out. ... and leads us ... to project ourselves, our emotions."<sup id="cite_ref-213"><a href="https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/2001:_A_Space_Odyssey#cite_note-213">[208]</a></sup>

### "A new heaven"\[[edit](https://en.wikipedia.org/w/index.php?title=2001:_A_Space_Odyssey&action=edit&section=28 "Edit section: "A new heaven"")\]

Clarke indicated his preferred reading of the ending of *2001* as oriented toward the creation of "a new heaven" provided by the Star Child.<sup id="cite_ref-auto1_197-3"><a href="https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/2001:_A_Space_Odyssey#cite_note-auto1-197">[192]</a></sup> His view was corroborated in a posthumously released interview with Kubrick.<sup id="cite_ref-SF-20180706_44-2"><a href="https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/2001:_A_Space_Odyssey#cite_note-SF-20180706-44">[44]</a></sup> Kubrick says that Bowman is elevated to a higher level of being that represents the next stage of human evolution. The film also conveys what some viewers have described as a sense of the [sublime](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sublime_(philosophy) "Sublime (philosophy)") and [numinous](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Numinous "Numinous").<sup id="cite_ref-Ebert_51-3"><a href="https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/2001:_A_Space_Odyssey#cite_note-Ebert-51">[50]</a></sup> Ebert writes in his essay on *2001* in *[The Great Movies](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/The_Great_Movies "The Great Movies")*:

[![](https://upload.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/en/thumb/0/09/2001child2.JPG/240px-2001child2.JPG)](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/File:2001child2.JPG)

The Star Child looking upon the Earth

> North's \[rejected\] score, which is available on a recording, is a good job of film composition, but would have been wrong for *2001* because, like all scores, it attempts to underline the action—to give us emotional cues. The classical music chosen by Kubrick exists outside the action. It uplifts. It wants to be sublime; it brings a seriousness and transcendence to the visuals.<sup id="cite_ref-Ebert_51-4"><a href="https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/2001:_A_Space_Odyssey#cite_note-Ebert-51">[50]</a></sup>

In a book on architecture, Gregory Caicco writes that *Space Odyssey* illustrates how our quest for space is motivated by two contradictory desires, a "desire for the sublime" characterised by a need to encounter something totally other than ourselves—"something numinous"—and the conflicting desire for a beauty that makes us feel no longer "lost in space", but at home.<sup id="cite_ref-214"><a href="https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/2001:_A_Space_Odyssey#cite_note-214">[209]</a></sup> Similarly, an article in *The Greenwood Encyclopedia of Science Fiction and Fantasy*, titled "Sense of Wonder", describes how *2001* creates a "numinous sense of wonder" by portraying a universe that inspires a sense of awe but that at the same time we feel we can understand.<sup id="cite_ref-215"><a href="https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/2001:_A_Space_Odyssey#cite_note-215">[210]</a></sup> Christopher Palmer wrote that "the sublime and the banal" coexist in the film, as it implies that to get into space, people had to suspend the "sense of wonder" that motivated them to explore it.<sup id="cite_ref-216"><a href="https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/2001:_A_Space_Odyssey#cite_note-216">[211]</a></sup>

### HAL's breakdown\[[edit](https://en.wikipedia.org/w/index.php?title=2001:_A_Space_Odyssey&action=edit&section=29 "Edit section: HAL's breakdown")\]

See also: [HAL](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Interpretations_of_2001:_A_Space_Odyssey#HAL "Interpretations of 2001: A Space Odyssey")

[![](https://upload.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/thumb/7/73/HAL9000_Case.svg/100px-HAL9000_Case.svg.png)](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/File:HAL9000_Case.svg)

One of HAL 9000's interfaces

The reasons for HAL's malfunction and subsequent malignant behaviour have elicited much discussion. He has been compared to [Frankenstein's monster](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Frankenstein%27s_monster "Frankenstein's monster"). In Clarke's novel, HAL malfunctions because of being ordered to lie to the crew of *Discovery* and withhold confidential information from them, namely the confidentially programmed mission priority over expendable human life, despite being constructed for "the accurate processing of information without distortion or concealment". This would not be addressed on film until the 1984 follow-up, *[2010: The Year We Make Contact](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/2010:_The_Year_We_Make_Contact "2010: The Year We Make Contact")*. Film critic Roger Ebert wrote that HAL, as the supposedly perfect computer, is actually the most human of the characters.<sup id="cite_ref-Ebert_51-5"><a href="https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/2001:_A_Space_Odyssey#cite_note-Ebert-51">[50]</a></sup> In an interview with Joseph Gelmis in 1969, Kubrick said that HAL "had an acute emotional crisis because he could not accept evidence of his own fallibility".<sup id="cite_ref-217"><a href="https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/2001:_A_Space_Odyssey#cite_note-217">[212]</a></sup>

### "Star Child" symbolism\[[edit](https://en.wikipedia.org/w/index.php?title=2001:_A_Space_Odyssey&action=edit&section=30 "Edit section: "Star Child" symbolism")\]

Multiple allegorical interpretations of *2001* have been proposed. The symbolism of life and death can be seen through the final moments of the film, which are defined by the image of the "Star Child", an [in utero](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Uterus "Uterus") foetus that draws on the work of [Lennart Nilsson](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lennart_Nilsson "Lennart Nilsson").<sup id="cite_ref-Burgoot2006p339_218-0"><a href="https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/2001:_A_Space_Odyssey#cite_note-Burgoot2006p339-218">[213]</a></sup> The Star Child signifies a "great new beginning",<sup id="cite_ref-Burgoot2006p339_218-1"><a href="https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/2001:_A_Space_Odyssey#cite_note-Burgoot2006p339-218">[213]</a></sup> and is depicted naked and ungirded but with its eyes wide open.<sup id="cite_ref-219"><a href="https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/2001:_A_Space_Odyssey#cite_note-219">[214]</a></sup> Leonard F. Wheat sees *2001* as a multi-layered allegory, commenting simultaneously on [Nietzsche](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Nietzsche "Nietzsche"), Homer, and the relationship of man to machine.<sup id="cite_ref-220"><a href="https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/2001:_A_Space_Odyssey#cite_note-220">[215]</a></sup> *Rolling Stone* reviewer Bob McClay sees the film as like a four-movement symphony, its story told with "deliberate realism".<sup id="cite_ref-221"><a href="https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/2001:_A_Space_Odyssey#cite_note-221">[216]</a></sup>

### Military satellites\[[edit](https://en.wikipedia.org/w/index.php?title=2001:_A_Space_Odyssey&action=edit&section=31 "Edit section: Military satellites")\]

Kubrick originally planned a voice-over to reveal that the satellites seen after the prologue are nuclear weapons,<sup id="cite_ref-Walker_pp181-182_222-0"><a href="https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/2001:_A_Space_Odyssey#cite_note-Walker_pp181-182-222">[217]</a></sup> and that the Star Child would detonate the weapons at the end of the film<sup id="cite_ref-223"><a href="https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/2001:_A_Space_Odyssey#cite_note-223">[218]</a></sup> but felt this would create associations with *Dr. Strangelove* and decided not to make it obvious that they were "war machines". A few weeks before the film's release, the U.S. and Soviet governments had agreed not to put any nuclear [weapons into outer space](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Militarisation_of_space "Militarisation of space").<sup id="cite_ref-Bizony_224-0"><a href="https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/2001:_A_Space_Odyssey#cite_note-Bizony-224">[219]</a></sup> In a book he wrote with Kubrick's assistance, Alexander Walker states that Kubrick eventually decided that nuclear weapons had "no place at all in the film's thematic development", being an "orbiting red herring" that would "merely have raised irrelevant questions to suggest this as a reality of the twenty-first century".<sup id="cite_ref-Walker_pp181-182_222-1"><a href="https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/2001:_A_Space_Odyssey#cite_note-Walker_pp181-182-222">[217]</a></sup>

Kubrick scholar [Michel Ciment](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Michel_Ciment "Michel Ciment"), discussing Kubrick's attitude toward human aggression and instinct, observes: "The bone cast into the air by the ape (now become a man) is transformed at the other extreme of civilization, by one of those abrupt ellipses characteristic of the director, into a spacecraft on its way to the moon."<sup id="cite_ref-225"><a href="https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/2001:_A_Space_Odyssey#cite_note-225">[220]</a></sup> In contrast to Ciment's reading of a cut to a serene "other extreme of civilization", science fiction novelist [Robert Sawyer](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Robert_J._Sawyer "Robert J. Sawyer"), in the Canadian documentary *2001 and Beyond*, says he sees it as a cut from a bone to a nuclear weapons platform, explaining that "what we see is not how far we've leaped ahead, what we see is that today, '2001', and four million years ago on the African veldt, it's exactly the same—the power of mankind is the power of its weapons. It's a continuation, not a discontinuity in that jump."<sup id="cite_ref-beyond_226-0"><a href="https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/2001:_A_Space_Odyssey#cite_note-beyond-226">[221]</a></sup>

### Influence\[[edit](https://en.wikipedia.org/w/index.php?title=2001:_A_Space_Odyssey&action=edit&section=32 "Edit section: Influence")\]

*2001: A Space Odyssey* is widely regarded as among the [greatest and most influential films](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/List_of_films_considered_the_best "List of films considered the best").<sup id="cite_ref-NYT-20180510_227-0"><a href="https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/2001:_A_Space_Odyssey#cite_note-NYT-20180510-227">[222]</a></sup> It is considered one of the major artistic works of the 20th century, with many critics and filmmakers considering it Kubrick's masterpiece.<sup id="cite_ref-228"><a href="https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/2001:_A_Space_Odyssey#cite_note-228">[223]</a></sup> In the 1980s,<sup id="cite_ref-229"><a href="https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/2001:_A_Space_Odyssey#cite_note-229">[224]</a></sup> critic [David Denby](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/David_Denby "David Denby") compared Kubrick to the monolith from *2001: A Space Odyssey*, calling him "a force of supernatural intelligence, appearing at great intervals amid high-pitched shrieks, who gives the world a violent kick up the next rung of the evolutionary ladder".<sup id="cite_ref-230"><a href="https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/2001:_A_Space_Odyssey#cite_note-230">[225]</a></sup> By the start of the 21st century, *2001: A Space Odyssey* had become recognised as among the best films ever made by such sources as the [British Film Institute](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/British_Film_Institute "British Film Institute") (BFI). *[The Village Voice](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/The_Village_Voice "The Village Voice")* ranked the film at number 11 in its Top 250 "Best Films of the Century" list in 1999, based on a poll of critics.<sup id="cite_ref-231"><a href="https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/2001:_A_Space_Odyssey#cite_note-231">[226]</a></sup> In January 2002, the film was included on the list of the "Top 100 Essential Films of All Time" by the [National Society of Film Critics](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/National_Society_of_Film_Critics "National Society of Film Critics").<sup id="cite_ref-Carr81_232-0"><a href="https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/2001:_A_Space_Odyssey#cite_note-Carr81-232">[227]</a></sup><sup id="cite_ref-233"><a href="https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/2001:_A_Space_Odyssey#cite_note-233">[228]</a></sup> *Sight & Sound* magazine ranked the film twelfth in its greatest films of all-time list in 1982,<sup id="cite_ref-234"><a href="https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/2001:_A_Space_Odyssey#cite_note-234">[229]</a></sup> tenth in 1992 critics' poll of greatest films,<sup id="cite_ref-235"><a href="https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/2001:_A_Space_Odyssey#cite_note-235">[230]</a></sup> sixth in the top ten films of all time in its 2002,<sup id="cite_ref-S&amp;S_236-0"><a href="https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/2001:_A_Space_Odyssey#cite_note-S&amp;S-236">[231]</a></sup> [2012](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/The_Sight_and_Sound_Greatest_Films_of_All_Time_2012#Critics'_poll "The Sight and Sound Greatest Films of All Time 2012")<sup id="cite_ref-S&amp;S2012_237-0"><a href="https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/2001:_A_Space_Odyssey#cite_note-S&amp;S2012-237">[232]</a></sup> and [2022](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/The_Sight_and_Sound_Greatest_Films_of_All_Time_2022#Critics'_poll "The Sight and Sound Greatest Films of All Time 2022") critics' polls editions;<sup id="cite_ref-S&amp;S2022_174-1"><a href="https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/2001:_A_Space_Odyssey#cite_note-S&amp;S2022-174">[169]</a></sup> it also tied for second and first place in the [magazine's 2012](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/The_Sight_%26_Sound_Greatest_Films_of_All_Time_2012#Directors'_poll "The Sight & Sound Greatest Films of All Time 2012")<sup id="cite_ref-S&amp;S2012_237-1"><a href="https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/2001:_A_Space_Odyssey#cite_note-S&amp;S2012-237">[232]</a></sup> and [2022 directors' poll](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/The_Sight_and_Sound_Greatest_Films_of_All_Time_2022#Directors'_poll "The Sight and Sound Greatest Films of All Time 2022").<sup id="cite_ref-S&amp;S2022_174-2"><a href="https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/2001:_A_Space_Odyssey#cite_note-S&amp;S2022-174">[169]</a></sup> The film was voted no. 43 on the list of "100 Greatest Films" by the prominent French magazine *[Cahiers du cinéma](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cahiers_du_cin%C3%A9ma "Cahiers du cinéma")* in 2008.<sup id="cite_ref-:2_238-0"><a href="https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/2001:_A_Space_Odyssey#cite_note-:2-238">[233]</a></sup> In 2010, *[The Guardian](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/The_Guardian "The Guardian")* named it "the best sci-fi and fantasy film of all time".<sup id="cite_ref-239"><a href="https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/2001:_A_Space_Odyssey#cite_note-239">[234]</a></sup> The film ranked 4th in [BBC](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/BBC "BBC")'s 2015 list of the 100 greatest American films.<sup id="cite_ref-240"><a href="https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/2001:_A_Space_Odyssey#cite_note-240">[235]</a></sup> In 1991, it was deemed "culturally, historically, or aesthetically significant" by the United States [Library of Congress](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Library_of_Congress "Library of Congress") and selected for preservation in the [National Film Registry](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/National_Film_Registry "National Film Registry").<sup id="cite_ref-241"><a href="https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/2001:_A_Space_Odyssey#cite_note-241">[236]</a></sup> In 2010, it was named the greatest film of all time by *[The Moving Arts Film Journal](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/The_Moving_Arts_Film_Journal "The Moving Arts Film Journal")*.<sup id="cite_ref-242"><a href="https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/2001:_A_Space_Odyssey#cite_note-242">[237]</a></sup>

> Stanley Kubrick made the ultimate science fiction movie, and it is going to be very hard for someone to come along and make a better movie, as far as I'm concerned. On a technical level, it \[*[Star Wars](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Star_Wars_(film) "Star Wars (film)")*\] can be compared, but personally I think that *2001* is far superior.

—George Lucas, 1977<sup id="cite_ref-in70mm_130-2"><a href="https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/2001:_A_Space_Odyssey#cite_note-in70mm-130">[126]</a></sup>

The influence of *2001* on subsequent filmmakers is considerable. [Steven Spielberg](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Steven_Spielberg "Steven Spielberg"), [George Lucas](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/George_Lucas "George Lucas"), and others—including many special effects technicians—discuss the impact the film has had on them in a featurette titled *Standing on the Shoulders of Kubrick: The Legacy of 2001*, included in the 2007 DVD release of the film. Spielberg calls it his film generation's "big bang", while Lucas says it was "hugely inspirational", calling Kubrick "the filmmaker's filmmaker". Director [Martin Scorsese](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Martin_Scorsese "Martin Scorsese") has listed it as one of his favourite films of all time.<sup id="cite_ref-243"><a href="https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/2001:_A_Space_Odyssey#cite_note-243">[238]</a></sup> [Sydney Pollack](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sydney_Pollack "Sydney Pollack") calls it "groundbreaking", and [William Friedkin](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/William_Friedkin "William Friedkin") says *2001* is "the grandfather of all such films". At the 2007 Venice film festival, director [Ridley Scott](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ridley_Scott "Ridley Scott") said he believed *2001* was the unbeatable film that in a sense killed the science fiction genre.<sup id="cite_ref-244"><a href="https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/2001:_A_Space_Odyssey#cite_note-244">[239]</a></sup> Similarly, film critic Michel Ciment in his essay "Odyssey of Stanley Kubrick" wrote, "Kubrick has conceived a film which in one stroke has made the whole science fiction cinema obsolete."<sup id="cite_ref-245"><a href="https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/2001:_A_Space_Odyssey#cite_note-245">[240]</a></sup>

Others credit *2001* with opening up a market for films such as *[Close Encounters of the Third Kind](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Close_Encounters_of_the_Third_Kind "Close Encounters of the Third Kind")*, *[Alien](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Alien_(film) "Alien (film)")*, *[Blade Runner](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Blade_Runner "Blade Runner")*, *[Contact](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Contact_(1997_American_film) "Contact (1997 American film)")*, and *[Interstellar](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Interstellar_(film) "Interstellar (film)")*, proving that big-budget "serious" science-fiction films can be commercially successful, and establishing the "sci-fi blockbuster" as a Hollywood staple.<sup id="cite_ref-246"><a href="https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/2001:_A_Space_Odyssey#cite_note-246">[241]</a></sup> Science magazine *Discover*'s blogger Stephen Cass, discussing the film's considerable impact on subsequent science fiction, writes that "the balletic spacecraft scenes set to sweeping classical music, the tarantula-soft tones of HAL 9000 and the ultimate alien artefact, the monolith, have all become enduring cultural icons in their own right."<sup id="cite_ref-247"><a href="https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/2001:_A_Space_Odyssey#cite_note-247">[242]</a></sup> Trumbull said that when working on *[Star Trek: The Motion Picture](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Star_Trek:_The_Motion_Picture "Star Trek: The Motion Picture")* he made a scene without dialogue because of "something I really learned with Kubrick and *2001*: Stop talking for a while, and let it all flow".<sup id="cite_ref-tifftrumbull_248-0"><a href="https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/2001:_A_Space_Odyssey#cite_note-tifftrumbull-248">[243]</a></sup>

Kubrick did not envision a sequel. Fearing the later exploitation and recycling of his material in other productions (as was done with the props from MGM's *Forbidden Planet*), he ordered all sets, props, miniatures, production blueprints, and prints of unused scenes destroyed.<sup id="cite_ref-249"><a href="https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/2001:_A_Space_Odyssey#cite_note-249">[244]</a></sup> Most of these materials were lost, with some exceptions: a *2001* spacesuit backpack appeared in the "Close Up" episode of the Gerry Anderson series *[UFO](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/UFO_(British_TV_series) "UFO (British TV series)")* and one of HAL's eyepieces is in the possession of the author of *Hal's Legacy*, David G. Stork.<sup id="cite_ref-Bizony_224-1"><a href="https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/2001:_A_Space_Odyssey#cite_note-Bizony-224">[219]</a></sup><sup id="cite_ref-2010featurette_250-0"><a href="https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/2001:_A_Space_Odyssey#cite_note-2010featurette-250">[245]</a></sup><sup id="cite_ref-251"><a href="https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/2001:_A_Space_Odyssey#cite_note-251">[246]</a></sup><sup id="cite_ref-252"><a href="https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/2001:_A_Space_Odyssey#cite_note-252">[247]</a></sup> In March 2015, a model of the Aries 1B Trans-Lunar Space Shuttle used in the film was sold at auction for $344,000 in the United States.<sup id="cite_ref-253"><a href="https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/2001:_A_Space_Odyssey#cite_note-253">[248]</a></sup> In 2012, Lockheed engineer Adam Johnson, working with [Frederick I. Ordway III](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Frederick_I._Ordway_III "Frederick I. Ordway III"), science adviser to Kubrick, wrote the book *2001: The Lost Science*, which for the first time featured many of the blueprints of the spacecraft and film sets that previously had been thought destroyed. Clarke wrote three sequel novels: *[2010: Odyssey Two](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/2010:_Odyssey_Two "2010: Odyssey Two")* (1982), *[2061: Odyssey Three](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/2061:_Odyssey_Three "2061: Odyssey Three")* (1987), and *[3001: The Final Odyssey](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/3001:_The_Final_Odyssey "3001: The Final Odyssey")* (1997). The only filmed sequel, *2010: The Year We Make Contact*, released in 1984, was based on Clarke's 1982 novel. Kubrick was not involved; it was directed as a spin-off by [Peter Hyams](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Peter_Hyams "Peter Hyams") in a more conventional style. The other two novels have not been adapted for the screen, although actor [Tom Hanks](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tom_Hanks "Tom Hanks") in June 1999 expressed a passing interest in possible adaptations.<sup id="cite_ref-254"><a href="https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/2001:_A_Space_Odyssey#cite_note-254">[249]</a></sup>

To commemorate the 50th anniversary of the film's release, an exhibit called "The Barmecide Feast" opened on 8 April 2018, in the Smithsonian Institution's [National Air and Space Museum](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/National_Air_and_Space_Museum "National Air and Space Museum"). The exhibit features a fully realised, full-scale reflection of the neo-classical hotel room from the film's penultimate scene.<sup id="cite_ref-255"><a href="https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/2001:_A_Space_Odyssey#cite_note-255">[250]</a></sup><sup id="cite_ref-256"><a href="https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/2001:_A_Space_Odyssey#cite_note-256">[251]</a></sup> Director [Christopher Nolan](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Christopher_Nolan "Christopher Nolan") presented a mastered 70 mm print of *2001* for the film's 50th anniversary at the [2018 Cannes Film Festival](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/2018_Cannes_Film_Festival "2018 Cannes Film Festival") on 12 May.<sup id="cite_ref-NYT-20180511_257-0"><a href="https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/2001:_A_Space_Odyssey#cite_note-NYT-20180511-257">[252]</a></sup><sup id="cite_ref-258"><a href="https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/2001:_A_Space_Odyssey#cite_note-258">[253]</a></sup> The new 70 mm print is a photochemical recreation made from the original camera negative, for the first time since the film's original theatrical run.<sup id="cite_ref-AT-20180721_259-0"><a href="https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/2001:_A_Space_Odyssey#cite_note-AT-20180721-259">[254]</a></sup><sup id="cite_ref-260"><a href="https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/2001:_A_Space_Odyssey#cite_note-260">[255]</a></sup> Further, an exhibit entitled "Envisioning 2001: Stanley Kubrick's Space Odyssey" presented at the [Museum of the Moving Image](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Museum_of_the_Moving_Image "Museum of the Moving Image") in [Astoria, Queens](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Astoria,_Queens "Astoria, Queens"), [New York City](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/New_York_City "New York City") opened in January 2020.<sup id="cite_ref-NYT-20200123_261-0"><a href="https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/2001:_A_Space_Odyssey#cite_note-NYT-20200123-261">[256]</a></sup>

In July 2020, a silver space suit was sold at auction in Los Angeles for $370,000, exceeding its estimate of $200,000–300,000. Four layers of paint indicate it was used in multiple scenes, including the Clavius Moon base sequence. The helmet had been painted green at one stage, leading to a belief that it may have been worn during the scene where Dave Bowman disconnects HAL 9000.<sup id="cite_ref-auction_262-0"><a href="https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/2001:_A_Space_Odyssey#cite_note-auction-262">[257]</a></sup>

Stanley Kubrick introduced Arthur C. Clarke to [Joseph Campbell](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Joseph_Campbell "Joseph Campbell")'s 1949 book *[The Hero with a Thousand Faces](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/The_Hero_with_a_Thousand_Faces "The Hero with a Thousand Faces")* during the writing of *2001: A Space Odyssey*. There are allegorical archetypal patterns of the "[hero's journey](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hero%27s_journey "Hero's journey")" in this film. Clarke called Campbell's book "very stimulating" in a diary entry.<sup id="cite_ref-263"><a href="https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/2001:_A_Space_Odyssey#cite_note-263">[258]</a></sup>

## Home media\[[edit](https://en.wikipedia.org/w/index.php?title=2001:_A_Space_Odyssey&action=edit&section=33 "Edit section: Home media")\]

The film has been released in several forms:

-   In 1980, [MGM/CBS Home Video](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/MGM/CBS_Home_Video "MGM/CBS Home Video") released the film on [VHS](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/VHS "VHS") and [Betamax](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Betamax "Betamax").<sup id="cite_ref-264"><a href="https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/2001:_A_Space_Odyssey#cite_note-264">[259]</a></sup>
-   In 1989, [The Criterion Collection](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/The_Criterion_Collection "The Criterion Collection") released a two-disc special [LaserDisc](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/LaserDisc "LaserDisc") edition with the transfer monitored by Kubrick himself.<sup id="cite_ref-265"><a href="https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/2001:_A_Space_Odyssey#cite_note-265">[260]</a></sup>
-   In 2008, [Warner Home Video](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Warner_Home_Video "Warner Home Video") released the film on Blu-ray.<sup id="cite_ref-266"><a href="https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/2001:_A_Space_Odyssey#cite_note-266">[261]</a></sup>
-   In 2018, Warner Bros. Home Entertainment re-released it on Blu-ray and [4K](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/4K_resolution "4K resolution") [HDR](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/High-dynamic-range_video "High-dynamic-range video") on [Ultra HD Blu-ray](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ultra_HD_Blu-ray "Ultra HD Blu-ray"), based on a [8K](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/8K_resolution "8K resolution") scan of the original camera negative and audio remixed and remastered in [DTS-HD MA 5.1](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/DTS-HD_Master_Audio "DTS-HD Master Audio").<sup id="cite_ref-4k_267-0"><a href="https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/2001:_A_Space_Odyssey#cite_note-4k-267">[262]</a></sup>

## Re-releases\[[edit](https://en.wikipedia.org/w/index.php?title=2001:_A_Space_Odyssey&action=edit&section=34 "Edit section: Re-releases")\]

The film was re-released in 1974, 1977, 1980<sup id="cite_ref-Hall_133-1"><a href="https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/2001:_A_Space_Odyssey#cite_note-Hall-133">[129]</a></sup> and 1993.<sup id="cite_ref-93rr_268-0"><a href="https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/2001:_A_Space_Odyssey#cite_note-93rr-268">[263]</a></sup> In 2001, a restoration of the 70 mm version was screened at the Ebert's Overlooked Film Festival, and the production was also reissued to selected film houses in North America, Europe and Asia.<sup id="cite_ref-269"><a href="https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/2001:_A_Space_Odyssey#cite_note-269">[264]</a></sup><sup id="cite_ref-270"><a href="https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/2001:_A_Space_Odyssey#cite_note-270">[265]</a></sup>

For the film's 50th anniversary, Warner Bros. struck new [70 mm](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/70_mm_film "70 mm film") prints from printing elements made directly from the original film negative.<sup id="cite_ref-AT-20180721_259-1"><a href="https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/2001:_A_Space_Odyssey#cite_note-AT-20180721-259">[254]</a></sup> This was done under the supervision of film director [Christopher Nolan](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Christopher_Nolan "Christopher Nolan"), who has spoken of *2001*'s influence on his career. Following a showing at the 2018 [Cannes Film Festival](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cannes_Film_Festival "Cannes Film Festival") introduced by Nolan, the film had a limited worldwide release at select 70 mm-equipped theatres in the summer of 2018,<sup id="cite_ref-NYT-20180511_257-1"><a href="https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/2001:_A_Space_Odyssey#cite_note-NYT-20180511-257">[252]</a></sup><sup id="cite_ref-271"><a href="https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/2001:_A_Space_Odyssey#cite_note-271">[266]</a></sup> followed by a one-week run in North American [IMAX](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/IMAX "IMAX") theatres (including five locations equipped with 70 mm IMAX projectors).<sup id="cite_ref-272"><a href="https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/2001:_A_Space_Odyssey#cite_note-272">[267]</a></sup>

On 3 December 2018, an [8K Ultra-high definition television](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ultra-high-definition_television "Ultra-high-definition television") version of the film was reported to have been broadcast in select theatres and shopping-mall demonstration stations in Japan.<sup id="cite_ref-VERGE-20181203_273-0"><a href="https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/2001:_A_Space_Odyssey#cite_note-VERGE-20181203-273">[268]</a></sup>
